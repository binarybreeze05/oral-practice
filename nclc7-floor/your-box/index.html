[examiner: "Hello?"]

Good morning, I'd like to know how much your introduction to paragliding costs.

[examiner: "It depends, we have several packages."]

In that case, which package would you recommend for a complete beginner who's only free on weekends?

[examiner: "For beginners, our one-day introduction, Saturdays or Sundays."]

Noted. Besides, if there were too much wind to take off, what would you do?

[examiner: "We'd postpone the flights to another date."]

So if I understand correctly, it all depends on the wind. Since I live an hour away by car, how far in advance would you warn me?

Okay. And during the mini-flights, will I be alone up there? Even if they're short, I'd like an instructor to be with me, at least the first time.

What your box must include

Formal call naming the paragliding ad: reactive turns with one C1 glimpse, varied structures and links, precise words, 58 clean seconds.

0 of 14 ticked

The frame

  • shared timing; business call, tu kept up is D8
    details
    • Start your box with the examiner's opening stage line, [examiner: "Hello?"], and time everything from it. Every Section A box uses the same format and the same timing basis, so your point can be measured. Time spent explaining the roles before that line does not count.“The clock starts at the examiner's first line of the section ("Hello?" in A, "Hi! How are you?" in B).”
    • Use vous and votre from your first sentence and keep polite conditionals in every request. Section A is a call to a business, so the frame needs the formal register from the start. Slipping into tu and keeping it up is disaster D8, wrong register.“*Vous* and *votre* from the first sentence, polite conditionals throughout.”
  • cheap frame; the scripted partial answer needs this probe
    details
    • Make your first turn a single sentence that greets formally, names the activity the ad offers, and asks your probe question. One sentence completes the frame (register set, ad identified) in the fewest words, and the words saved go to evidence later, because the box sits at the time floor. Keep hard grammar out of this turn: raters believe the opener least.“Turn 1 names the ad, sets *vous*, and asks the probe.”
    • Make your probe the question the examiner's booklet scripts a partial answer for (here, the price), asked as "how much" rather than "what". The examiner's partial answer ("it depends, we have several packages") is what your second turn reacts to, and reacting to a partial answer is the B2 rung. Without the probe there is nothing to react to and the task condition fails.“Without the price question, no partial answer comes.”

The task moves

  • proves you heard them; steering earns the B2 rung
    details
    • Open your reply to the "it depends" answer with an audible uptake from it: a consequence connector plus the examiner's own word for the offer (package, formula, option), never "which one". The rater has to hear that this turn could only follow their reply. Saying their noun with your own quel / quelle avoids lequel, which must agree with a noun you may not have caught; if they name no noun, ask what they would recommend.“The reply to the partial answer must audibly take something from it: a connector ("In that case"), the examiner's own noun ("which package"), or a reword.”
    • In that same turn, turn their vague answer into a precise request anchored in a fact about you (the built box uses your level and when you are free). A request tied to your situation steers the exchange, which is what lifts a reaction to the B2 rung. Without the anchor the advice question turns generic and could follow any ad.“The request has no anchor in your situation.”
  • price, length, kit, place fit any ad
    details
    • Ask at least two "only here" questions, ones that make sense only for paragliding, drawn from what the activity involves: wind, taking off, landing, flying alone or with an instructor, weight limits. Price, length, equipment and location fit any ad, so they carry no "only here" credit. Questions that belong to this activity show you are handling this call, not reciting a general list.“The only-here material has to come from what paragliding actually involves: wind, taking off, landing, flying alone or with an instructor, weight limits.”
  • its gist is predictable, so restating is safe
    details
    • Make one only-here question a keystone, a question whose answer has a gist you can predict, so your next turn can restate it safely (the built box picks wind, because any answer to a wind question admits the flights depend on the weather on the day). The C1 glimpse has to be reactive and safe at the same time, and you can only restate a gist you can predict. A less certain gist, such as a reassuring reply, can make a prepared restatement come out wrong.“You can only restate a gist you can predict, and for paragliding one gist is close to certain”
    • Ask your keystone question as a hypothetical about the one real risk of this activity, using si + imperfect + conditional, with a structure such as trop de... pour + infinitive attached if it fits. The hypothetical turns a stock si clause into evidence tied to this call, and it is the answer your next turn restates. Make it a direct question about what they would do: a "what would happen" question forces the qu'est-ce qui / que choice.“a *si* hypothetical about the one real risk of this activity”
  • the C1 glimpse; either half alone stays B2
    details
    • In the turn after the keystone answer, restate its gist and, in the same turn, draw a consequence for your own situation as a question you could not have asked before hearing it (the built box uses how far you live from the site; other situation facts the source names are never having flown, a fear of heights, being free only at weekends). This is the C1 glimpse: a well-judged question that combines their answer with your situation. The restatement alone is cheap, and dropping either half leaves the move at plain B2, the coin-flip zone between 6 and 7.“Fix made: the cause clause sits in the same turn as the restatement, so the restatement is used right away.”

Language evidence

  • range lifts to upper B2; conditionals alone don't count
    details
    • Use at least four different marked structure types, each correct, with at least one inside a turn that replies to the examiner (the built box has five: an indirect question with inversion, a relative clause with ne... que, si + imperfect + conditional, trop de... pour + infinitive, and an audible subjunctive). A marked structure is a grammar form only stronger speakers use reliably, and range across types is what separates the upper B2 box from the lower one. The future and polite conditionals are not counted, so a box built on conditionals alone sounds like correct common complex sentences, which is lower B2.“Syntax: at least four (A) or five (B) different marked structure types, used correctly, at least one inside a reactive turn; at least three kinds of link (P8b).”
    • Put your solid structures in the turns that react to the examiner, starting with the reply to the partial answer, and keep the hard ones out of the opener. Evidence in the opener is discounted as rehearsed, while a structure inside a reply turn shows you building it live. The built box carries its restriction (ne... que) in the reply to "it depends" for this reason.“All the hard grammar sits in turns 2 to 5, none in the opener.”
    • Keep at most one hard structure in any sentence, and never ask for two mood choices that depend on each other. Two hard forms in one sentence is where a mid-sentence pause or a wrong form comes from, and that costs delivery. A politeness conditional is a free choice; only the subjunctive depends on another word.“No sentence holds more than one hard one.”
  • -er forms sound indicative and prove nothing
    details
    • Include one audible subjunctive, written as English whose natural French can only come out audible (soit, fasse, puisse, aille, ait, vienne, prenne). A regular -er verb sounds the same in the subjunctive and the indicative in je / tu / il / ils, so it proves nothing on the recording. Shape the English as a wish about what someone else is or does, so it produces que + subjunctive by itself; "to have an X" gives avoir and no subjunctive.“A line counted as carrying a subjunctive must be English whose natural French can only come out audible”
  • links are a condition; si counts as syntax
    details
    • Join clauses or turns with connectors from at least three different relations, and aim for four with a concession among them (the built box uses consequence, addition, cause and concession). Links are their own condition of the point. Plain "and / but / because" do not count, and an "if" clause is syntax, not a link, so it can't be counted twice; a fourth relation gives you margin if one fails.“The links cover four relations: consequence (*dans ce cas*, *donc*), addition (*par ailleurs*), cause (*comme*) and concession (*même si*).”
  • proves lexis; no clichés; English words risk D4
    details
    • Use about five or more precise topic words that the ad does not print and that your English produces by itself (options the source lists: moniteur, vent, décoller, atterrir, voile, casque, poids, biplace, pente école, radio, reporter, acompte, prévenir, à l'avance). Precise words prove lexis; everyday words (advise, free), words printed on the ad and words the examiner supplies do not count. Use no clichés and no English words: English inside French sentences more than once is disaster D4.“Lexis: about five (A) or six (B) precise words not on the ad, no clichés, no English words; in B, one natural informal expression.”
    • Choose each English word so that it produces the French word you are counting (for example "warn" gives prévenir, where "let me know" would not). The English is the box, so a precise word counts only if your English produces it on its own. A translator note may choose between natural renderings but never overrule the English; if the English pulls the wrong way, change the English.“Count a precise word only if the English produces it on its own.”

Control and credibility

  • say their real word; ignoring them is D7
    details
    • Open each new question after an examiner reply with a short acknowledgment of what they just said (one word is enough). A question that skips over their reply is what a recited list sounds like, and credibility is one of the five conditions of the point. A learned line that ignores the examiner more than once is disaster D7.“Without them your next question ignores their reply, and that is what a recited list sounds like.”
    • Drill the lines that echo the examiner as frames, not fixed sentences: repeat the noun they used in your uptake, and repeat their key word (wind, weather, conditions) in your restatement. The box can only write the most likely version of what they say. A frame lets you say their actual word, so the line never ignores what was said.“Lines to drill as frames, not fixed sentences (standard 13)”
  • handling the unexpected is B2 evidence itself
    details
    • For every examiner reply, test your next line against a helpful, a partial and a surprising version, and prepare a fallback for each that keeps the same structures and length. Your lines must still work whatever they say, or the box sounds recited. Handling an unexpected answer is B2 evidence in itself, and if a reply already answered your next question, swap that question instead of asking what was just answered.“Each examiner reply is followed by a line that still works whatever they say (see the reply table).”
  • control presumed after ~58 s; swap dead, never cut
    details
    • Land clear evidence in at least three separate turns, at least two of them replies to what the examiner said (the built box has four such turns, two of them replies). Replication shows the evidence is a pattern, not one lucky sentence. Replies weigh most because a turn that only makes sense after their answer cannot have been memorised.“Three moments of clear evidence in three turns, two of them replies (P6).”
    • Reach about 100 English words of your own speech, and if your reactive core is shorter, add a turn that earns its time with evidence (the built box's last turn carries a second only-here question, the concession and the subjunctive). The rater presumes control only after about 58 seconds of clean speech, so before that the box ends before the point. A turn added only for time adds seconds with nothing in them.“it must earn that time with evidence: a second only-here question, the box's only audible subjunctive and its concession link.”
    • Test every piece of your box by taking it out, and at the floor swap a piece that carries nothing for one that carries evidence (a precise word, a link, an uptake) instead of deleting it. Each piece must hold one of the five conditions or the time floor. Below the floor, cutting does not move the point earlier; the box just ends before it.“So in a box at the floor, a dead word is swapped for evidence (a precise word, a link, an uptake), not deleted; above the floor, dead words go.”
  • nothing forces a pause; invented facts get challenged
    details
    • Keep every sentence to about 18 French words or fewer, in one or two breath groups that pause at commas, with no rare word. Delivery is proved when nothing in the box forces a pause. A word you can't say reliably creates exactly the hesitation the box is there to avoid.“No sentence runs past about 18 French words; no word is rare.”
    • State as fact only what the ad prints or your own situation, and ask anything else as a question or hedge it. An unhedged invented fact is a target for the examiner. The built box takes "short" from the ad's own "mini", so it claims nothing new.“Anything the ad doesn't print is hedged ("probably", "I'd be surprised if"), asked as a question, or cut.”

Length

About 100 English words (about 58 seconds); the point lands roughly 1:20 to 1:30 after "Hello?".

After the point

  • Keep linked follow-ups going ~3 minutes; close on their signal: thanks, clear, calling back
    details
    • Keep asking linked questions for the remaining three minutes or so, each one following up on the last answer.
    • Close only when the examiner signals the end: thank them, say it is clear, and say you will check your dates and call back to book.
  • Reserves: height, radio, weight or age, gear, shoes, group size, insurance, parking, rates, deposit, refund
    details
    • Reserve topics the source lists: how high the mini-flights go and whether they guide you by radio; a weight or age limit; whether the gear (wing, helmet) is provided and what shoes to wear; how many students per instructor; insurance or a medical certificate; where the take-off site is and whether there is parking; how long the exceptional rates last (the ad mentions them); whether booking needs a deposit and whether you would be refunded if you cancelled.
  • Dry spell: restate ground training then flights; ask whether wind cancels the training
    details
    • If the call runs dry, restate the ad's printed programme order (the flights come after the ground training) and ask whether you would still do the ground training if the wind rose that day.
  • Sound like plain NCLC 6; slips are fine, patterns (D2 to D5, D7, D8) hurt
    details
    • Sound like a solid NCLC 6 on a quiet day: plain, correct sentences, still asking, still answering, no long silences, no English. Reusing the box's structures, a few gender or agreement slips, quick self-corrections, one request to repeat and one question they had already answered are not disasters.
    • What does pull the score down is a pattern: the examiner restarting you twice in a row or the call ending well before 5 minutes (D5), the same basic error in most turns (D2), long pauses or switching to English (D3, D4), learned lines that ignore what they said (D7), or tu kept up (D8).

Traps

  • Bonjour, not Salut; "since" as comme; "besides" as par ailleurs, not aussi
    details
    • Greet with "Good morning" so the French is Bonjour, never Salut.
    • "Since" as comme, not puisque: the examiner doesn't already know your reason. "Besides" as par ailleurs or en plus, not aussi, which means "so" at the start of a sentence.
  • No indirect "what"; their noun with quel/quelle, never "which one" (lequel)
    details
    • No indirect "what" ("I'd like to know what..."): it invites qu'est-ce que in an indirect question. Use "how much" or another question word. A direct "what would you do" is fine; "what would happen" forces the qu'est-ce qui / que choice.
    • Say the examiner's noun with your own quel / quelle (quel forfait, quelle formule, quelle option), not "which one", which needs lequel to agree with a noun you may not have caught.
  • Ad words never count: parapente, initiation, formation au sol, mini-vols; route too
    details
    • Ad words never count as precise: parapente, initiation, en route pour les nuages, découvrez de nouvelles sensations, au programme, formation au sol, essais pratiques, séries de mini-vols, réservez au, tarifs exceptionnels. Route is printed, so une heure de route reuses an ad word (à une heure de voiture does not). A word like mini-vols can still anchor a question; it just isn't evidence.
  • No -er subjunctive in je/tu/il/ils; "to have an X" gives none
    details
    • A subjunctive from a regular -er verb in je / tu / il / ils is inaudible and proves nothing. Make the English produce que + an audible form; "to have an X" gives avoir and no subjunctive.
  • Front-load concessions so pronouns stay clear; drill feriez, préviendriez, hard sentences
    details
    • Watch pronoun reference: a clause placed after "with me" can make its pronoun point to the wrong noun, so put a concession like that at the front of the sentence.
    • Drill the irregular conditionals and the hard sentences until smooth (feriez, préviendriez or vous m'avertiriez, s'il y avait trop de vent pour décoller, the même si... j'aimerais que... soit sentence).
  • Instructor as moniteur; mind débutant/e, seul/e; fix English, not translator notes
    details
    • Mind gender endings on yourself: débutant / débutante, seul / seule.
    • "Instructor" as moniteur, the usual word at a paragliding school (instructeur is also correct and precise).
    • A translator note only chooses between natural renderings. If your English doesn't produce the French you want, change the English.

CANOE-CAMPING TRIP

The prompt, English | French
Prompt

You have read this travel agency advertisement. You are talking about it to a friend with whom you want to go on vacation.

CANOE-CAMPING TRIP

THE TRAVEL AGENCY VACANCES NATURE IS ORGANIZING A WEEK OF « CANOE-CAMPING »

Paddle by day and sleep comfortably in a tent at night.

Experience moments of calm, but also of challenge and adventure! Discover the most beautiful landscapes of the region and fill up on fresh air!

All reservations possible at: www.vacancesnature.ca

Consigne

Vous avez lu cette publicité d’agence de voyage. Vous en parlez à un(e) ami(e) avec qui vous voulez partir en vacances.

SÉJOUR CANOT CAMPING

L'AGENCE DE VOYAGE VACANCES NATURE ORGANISE UNE SEMAINE EN « CANOT-CAMPING »

Pagayez de jour et dormez confortablement dans une tente le soir.

Vivez des instants de calme, mais aussi de dépassement et d'aventure ! Découvrez les plus beaux paysages de la région et faites le plein d'air pur !

Toutes les réservations possibles sur : www.vacancesnature.ca

The box we built (reference)128 words

[examiner: "Hi! How are you?"]

Great! I've found our vacation. It's a week of canoe-camping organized by an agency, Vacances Nature. We paddle during the day, and in the evening we probably pitch the tent on the shore. What I like is that it suits both of us: you want rest; me, I need to move. You're going to tell me that a tent isn't your thing.

[examiner: "Ha! Comfortably, in a tent? I really doubt it."]

I'm not surprised you're skeptical. But with a good inflatable mattress and a warm sleeping bag, you sleep well. Besides, after a whole day in the canoe, you'll fall asleep in two minutes.

[examiner: "A whole day in a canoe? I'm not sporty, I'll be exhausted!"]

Not sporty? That's exactly why it's perfect: unlike a hike, it's the canoe that carries the bags, not your back. And even if you were exhausted, I'd row for both of us.

Your box0 words · built 128
What your box must include

Present the canoe-camping ad as your shared trip, then defend it through two objections you steer, all five conditions met by reply 2's end.

0 of 14 ticked

The frame

  • shows you heard them; aims it at this friend
    details
    • Answer the friend's greeting with a single word, then go straight into the ad (if the friend says they are tired, a quick sympathetic word can turn that into an extra hook for the ad). Ignoring the greeting makes your first line sound launched from a script, which costs credibility (condition 5). One word shows you heard them, and anything more only delays the ad.“Greeting in B: one word”
    • In your first sentence about the ad, tie it to the trip you and the friend are already planning, so it lands as your shared plan and not as a leaflet you happened to see. The prompt sets up a friend you already want to travel with, and "present the document to a friend" means presenting it to this person. Without the tie, the presentation is no longer aimed at the friend and the frame (condition 1) is weaker.“ties the ad to an open plan the two of you share”
  • frame needs own words; vous with a friend fails
    details
    • Identify the ad by saying who organizes it, what kind of trip it is and how long it lasts, reshaping the ad's own sentence instead of reading it (the built box turned the ad's active sentence round into a participle used as a passive). The frame is only done when the ad is identified and presented in your own words, not read. Reshaping the printed sentence is what makes it rewording, and the passive participle also gives you one of your five structure types.“The ad is not identified: who organizes it, what, how long (C1).”
    • Speak to the friend with tu and on, in an informal register, and get your first tu in within roughly the first 15 seconds. This is a conversation with a friend, and vous kept up with a friend is a disaster on its own (D8). Setting tu early is also why a filler opener such as "Listen" could be cut.“Register: tu, on, informal markers.”

The task moves

  • ad is short; order and fit make it detailed
    details
    • Give the presentation a clear order in four steps: what the trip is, how a day goes (reword the ad's day-and-evening rhythm), why it fits the two of you, and the objection you expect back. Section B asks for a detailed presentation, and the ad is only four lines, so the detail has to come from this order and not from new facts. A one-sentence presentation is the B1 "simple presentation", not the detailed one.“a presentation in your own words with a clear order (what it is, how a day goes, why it suits the two of you, what you expect to hear back)”
    • When you say why it fits the two of you, illustrate it at once by matching what the ad offers to each of you differently (the built box mapped the ad's calm onto the friend and its challenge onto you; a fuller portrait of the friend was rejected because it costs words, leans on an unprinted fact and the examiner can deny it). The presentation has to say why the trip fits the two of you (P17b), and a bare claim that it suits you both is the B1 "arguments clear but little developed". Matching the ad's own promises to each of you holds up even if the friend denies the portrait, because the ad's promise still stands.“P17b wants the presentation to say why it fits the two of you”
  • examiner takes it, so reply 1 is preparable
    details
    • End the presentation by naming the one weak spot the ad itself invites, as what you expect the friend to tell you, with no question after it; pick it from comfort, effort, safety, weather, insects, time, price or boredom, choosing the one the ad's wording practically prints (the built box chose comfort, because the ad promises you will sleep comfortably in a tent). The examiner is told to leave you the initiative and to disagree, so they will almost always take a ready-made objection that is surely on their list, and your first reply becomes one you can prepare. Naming two at once forces a speech-length reply and leaves the second objection to chance, and a question after the hand-over invites something you did not plan (standard 3).“The presentation ends by naming the objection the ad invites (the tent).”
  • answered with no invented fact; objection 2 predictable
    details
    • In the first reply, grant the friend's doubt, then give one concrete counter to it (the built box answered comfort with gear you bring yourself, not something the agency is said to provide). A reply with no concrete counter leaves the objection unanswered, so the task criterion's "illustrated" fails. Making the counter something you bring keeps it from turning into an invented fact about the trip.“Reply 1 has no concrete counter, so the comfort objection is unanswered (C2 task: illustrated).”
    • End the first reply with a second reason, joined by an addition link, that invites the next objection you already have a reply for (the built box said the long day on the water would make the friend sleep at once, which invites "I'll be exhausted"). This line makes the second objection predictable: without it you need about four prepared second replies instead of one, and the addition link goes. It is the cheapest piece to cut, and cutting it still breaks a condition.“Without it the second objection is no longer called (four prepared second replies instead of one) and the addition link goes.”
    • Put one hard structure in each objection reply: an audible subjunctive in the first (after a feeling such as being surprised or understanding that), and même si + imperfect + conditional in the second. At least one structure has to sit inside a reactive turn, and with one in each reply the syntax count does not depend on which objection comes. A reply is also the least predictable place in the box, so a structure there is the hardest to dismiss as recited.“Every fallback keeps one hard structure in each reply (an audible subjunctive in reply 1, *même si* + imperfect + conditional in reply 2)”
  • most arguments fit any outdoor trip
    details
    • Keep your only-here argument, one that works for this trip and for no other outdoor trip, out of the presentation and use it in the second reply, with a consequence link that turns the friend's objection into the reason to go (the built box compared it with a hiking trip: here the boat takes the load off you). Most arguments for this ad fit any outdoor trip, so the task needs one only this trip allows. Spent in the presentation, it is gone when the objection it answers best arrives, and you end up repeating yourself, the booklet's named weakness.“Most arguments for this ad (nature, disconnecting, adventure, a change of scene) fit any outdoor trip.”
  • shows B2 determination; cannot be recited unheard
    details
    • End the second reply with a firm commitment, pushed further than the first reply, inside an "even if" hypothetical built on the friend's own word (the built box offered to do the hard part for the friend; the fallbacks use a worry that would fade after a day or two, or what you would do instead if it happened). The B2 column names defending clearly and with determination, and a commitment shows it where a joke would not. Putting the friend's own word inside the hypothetical means the line cannot be recited without having heard them.“Together they are the 'defends clearly and with determination' the B2 column names.”
    • Put one hard structure in each objection reply: an audible subjunctive in the first (after a feeling such as being surprised or understanding that), and même si + imperfect + conditional in the second. At least one structure has to sit inside a reactive turn, and with one in each reply the syntax count does not depend on which objection comes. A reply is also the least predictable place in the box, so a structure there is the hardest to dismiss as recited.“Every fallback keeps one hard structure in each reply (an audible subjunctive in reply 1, *même si* + imperfect + conditional in reply 2)”

Language evidence

  • B syntax and link minimums; and/but/because don't count
    details
    • Use five different types of marked structure (forms only stronger speakers use reliably), correctly, counting a family once (two clefts are one type); the built box used a participle as a passive, a cleft, a stressed pronoun put up front, an audible subjunctive, and même si + imperfect + conditional. Five types is the Section B minimum for the syntax criterion, so in a box at five none can go. Two sentences from the same family prove one type, not two.“at least four (A) or five (B) different marked structure types, used correctly, at least one inside a reactive turn”
    • Put one hard structure in each objection reply: an audible subjunctive in the first (after a feeling such as being surprised or understanding that), and même si + imperfect + conditional in the second. At least one structure has to sit inside a reactive turn, and with one in each reply the syntax count does not depend on which objection comes. A reply is also the least predictable place in the box, so a structure there is the hardest to dismiss as recited.“Every fallback keeps one hard structure in each reply (an audible subjunctive in reply 1, *même si* + imperfect + conditional in reply 2)”
    • Join clauses with links of three different relations, one of them a concession (the built box used addition, consequence and concession; contrast and cause are the other options). Under the strict count only connectors that join clauses or turns count: plain and, but and because don't, and neither does a preposition before a noun such as contrairement à. Section B needs three relations with a concession among them, and the box found two of its three by swapping words it already had, at no cost.“In Section B one of the three must be a concession.”
  • ad words are topic vocabulary; B wants an idiom
    details

Traps

  • Participles and adjectives agree with their real noun, not the nearest one
    details
    • A past participle or adjective agrees with the noun it describes (réservée with la dépendance, risquée with habitude); a passive participle agrees with its real subject, not the nearest noun (conçues with plateformes, not pulled to TikTok), and conçues keeps its cedilla.
  • quel que soit: three words, quel agrees, soit singular; si + imperfect, never conditional
    details
    • quel que soit: three words, quel agreeing with the noun (masculine âge), soit singular.
    • After si, the imperfect, never the conditional (rangeaient, keeping the e after g); the conditional goes in the other clause (suivraient).
  • grand (not grande) temps que + visible subjunctive; ce qui les rend; elide qu'ils, qu'à
    details
    • il est grand temps que: grand, not grande; pick a verb whose subjunctive differs from the present (prennent, double n), not se rendent compte.
    • Pronoun before the verb in ce qui les rend; elision in ce qu'ils voient and qu'à.
  • ses/ces, met/mets, a/à, et/est; one slip thrice is a disaster
    details
    • ses (his, her) versus ces (these); met, not mets, in the third person; a versus à, et versus est. The same slip three times is a disaster.
  • Spell Certes, développement, plateformes, vulnérables, conçues, rangeaient, du mal à, leur exemple, n'est pas
    details
    • Spelling the built box flagged: Certes, développement (double p, one l), plateformes (one word), vulnérables (plural), aurait du mal à (du, and à with its accent), leur exemple (singular), n'est pas.
  • No tu, slang, SMS, English; no reversal (except a concession), drift or half-sentence
    details

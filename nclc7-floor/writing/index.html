The question

What’s the LEAST amount of things I can say in the response for this prompt, such that after I have said that, the examiner would go: “surely i can’t give this kid less than an NCLC 7/B2+?????”. Like past that point, the rest of my response would have to be DISASTEROUS for me to score any below an NCLC 7/ B2+ . What’s that point exactly? And what’s the least amount of words that point can be?

The same question for writing: the least you can write, from the first word, after which a grader can’t place you below NCLC 7 unless the rest of the text is a disaster. One prompt from each of the 12 consolidated writing families (the most frequent prompt of each). For each: the point (where in the text it falls), the box (every word you write up to that point, in English), its word count, then why that box and why nothing less. Built the same way as the speaking page: three subagents derived the first principles for writing from scratch, one subagent per prompt built its box, and reviewer subagents attacked every box.

What came out

The answer. Section A: 59 to 71 English words, two paragraphs of four sentences, written in about 6 or 7 of the 25 minutes. That is still under the 80-word minimum: the rest is two or three plain closing sentences that take the story to about 90 to 100 French words. Section B: 95 to 116 English words, five or six short paragraphs (the salutation, your position with a concession, three arguments each with its reason, one of them developed), written in about 11 to 13 of the 35 minutes. The rest, about 110 to 130 plain French words, is planned sentence by sentence in each box's working so the letter reaches about 220. The table below gives each prompt's count.

Why the bar is higher than "B2". Same as speaking. On the publisher's 699-point scale, writing NCLC 7 is 428 to 471, inside B2 (400 to 499): a "just B2" text is NCLC 6. Two graders mark each text independently and give the same level less than half the time, so the box aims at upper B2.

What sets the length. In Section A, the content the printed start promises (what the dog found, how the man got to the wedding, how the prisoner was caught), four clean sentences so a grader takes your control as given, and the genre's own structures. There is no speaking-style time floor, which is why the A boxes are shorter than the speaking ones. In Section B, the instruction "develop at least three arguments": a list of three nouns proves the count but not "develop", so each argument has its reason inside the box.

Where the proof sits. The first sentence answers the reader's open question (A: what happened next; B: your position). The genre carries the structures: in a news story the past-before-past, the news-style conditional ("reportedly") and a quote with the speech tag after it; in a letter the concession, two subjunctives you can see on the page and a conditional. About five precise words the prompt doesn't print, no word repeated, and no error in the box: graders weigh spelling and vocabulary errors early and let them colour everything else.

What the box assumes about you. After the point you need to write like a clean NCLC 6: plain, correct sentences in the same tenses and register, no new hard structures, and enough of them to clear the minimum by about 10%. The box can't rescue a rest full of basic errors.

What is least certain. The grid's weights, how the two sections combine, how the typed test counts words, and what happens to a text under the minimum are not public. Each box's working says what it does if a grader is stricter than expected.

A. The facts everything stands on

F1. The tasks, as set. [source: CCI Paris Île-de-France (Le français des affaires), TEF – Expression écrite, exemples de sujets, 2024]

  • Section A, 25 minutes: "Voici le début d'un article de presse. Terminez cet article : en ajoutant à la suite un texte de 80 mots minimum ; en faisant plusieurs paragraphes."

  • Section B, 35 minutes: "Vous avez lu dans un journal l'affirmation suivante : « … » Écrivez une lettre au journal pour dire ce que vous en pensez (200 mots minimum). Développez au moins 3 arguments pour défendre votre point de vue."

  • Each section's time is locked: minutes saved in A can't be spent in B, and you can't go back to A. The test is typed; accents come from an on-screen keyboard if yours lacks them. No dictionary. [source: Livret du surveillant TEF 2025; Livret du responsable TEF 2026]

  • The publisher's own advice: in A, don't copy, summarise or rephrase the printed start, add new information; in B, the letter form is expected but a letterhead earns nothing, and generic introductions and conclusions that would fit any topic are ignored. [source: Le français des affaires blog, 2021, sections A and B]

F2. Who grades and on what. Two trained graders mark each script independently, centrally in Paris; strong disagreements are arbitrated; no re-marking. [source: exemples de sujets 2024; TEF Canada results page] The analytic grid has five criteria marked 0 to 10: task A, task B, syntax, lexicon, coherence/cohesion, combined with fixed, unpublished weights in which the two task criteria weigh the most. [source: Casanova, Aw & Demeuse 2024, the publisher's own researchers] The public list of what graders look at: respect of the situation, overall organisation, clarity of the information or argument, the ability to develop, nuance and make precise; vocabulary; syntax (sentences, tenses and moods); spelling. Their guiding questions are ladders: information "sketchy, vague, precise, detailed"; sentences "simple, complex, controlled"; "are tenses and moods used correctly?"; "is everyday spelling mastered?"; "is the vocabulary accurate, precise, appropriate?" [source: exemples de sujets 2024]

F3. How graders behave in practice (the publisher's 2021 data, about 35,000 scripts). The five criterion marks move together (one factor explains 92% of their variance): graders form one global level. The two graders give the same CEFR level only 45% of the time and levels at most one apart 92% of the time. The features that best predict their level are lexical: how advanced the words are, the share of correctly spelled words, the number of different words. [source: Casanova, Aw & Demeuse 2024]

F4. Where NCLC 7 sits. IRCC uses the "old score" column: writing NCLC 6 = 271-309, NCLC 7 = 310-348, NCLC 8 = 349-370. The certificate's main score is out of 699: since 11 December 2023, writing NCLC 7 = 428-471, inside B2 = 400-499. The publisher's older 0-450 table groups NCLC 6-7 as B2. [source: IRCC language-testing page; CCI NCLC and CEFR correspondence tables] However you read the tables, "just B2" is NCLC 6; NCLC 7 is a secure B2. With two noisy graders (F3), a text needs to deserve about the top of NCLC 7 or the bottom of NCLC 8 (upper B2) to stay at 7 or above about 9 times in 10. [reasoned: a noise model fitted to the published agreement rates]

F5. What separates a 6 from a 7 (NCLC/CLB writing 6 vs 7, CEFR B1 vs B2, paraphrased):

[source: Jansen et al. 2021; Vögelin et al. 2019, 2020; Trüb et al. 2026] Copied prompt wording and generic memorised text don't distinguish levels, and raters learn to recognise templates. B. The principles

P1. The verdict forms early, then gets defended. A grader forms a level within the first lines and then reads the rest through it; evidence against it has to be conspicuous to move it. That is why a point exists: after it, only conspicuous trouble counts. [reasoned from F6]

P2. Criteria add up evidence in different ways.

  • Gates (pass or fail on the whole text): the word minimums, several paragraphs (A), a letter to the paper (B), three arguments (B), on topic, the right genre. A gate left open can fail in the rest.

  • Range (seen once, proven): a complex structure, a tense or mood, a precise word, a nuance. One correct instance proves it.

  • Control (a rate): errors per stretch of text. It can never be banked; the box can only make it the grader's default belief.

  • Coverage (development): precise, detailed content.

  • Shape: introduction, development, ending. The box closes every gate that can close early, proves every range item, supplies the coverage, and sets control as the default. [reasoned]

P3. Put the gates inside the box, not promises. If the box only promises something ("for three reasons…"), an ordinary weak rest can break the promise, and a broken instruction is the most conspicuous negative evidence there is. If the box already contains it, the rest can only undo it by contradicting it, which is a disaster by definition. [reasoned]

P4. Zero errors in the box, and inoculate. Early errors weigh more, spelling errors spread by halo, and an error in the box makes a later repeat look systematic. You can reread, so a clean box is achievable. Then: put correct instances of the basic forms your plainer rest will lean on (a plural -ent verb, a passé composé with être and visible agreement, a feminine adjective, a/à) inside the box. A later slip on a form shown correctly earlier reads as a slip, not a gap. [reasoned from F5, F6]

P5. One correct complex structure beats two attempted ones with one wrong. NCLC 6 attempts complex structures and misses; NCLC 7 gets them right. A wrong complex sentence is evidence for 6. For vocabulary it is the reverse: show several precise words, because range itself is what is judged. [reasoned from F5]

P6. Only visible features count. In writing the grader sees forms, not intentions. Il faut que chacun limite looks like the present, so it proves no subjunctive; qu'il prenne, que ce soit, que nous fassions do. Past-participle agreement shows only with feminine or plural nouns. Use the switch: for a feature you control, choose the visible form; for one you're unsure of, choose the invisible form (a masculine singular noun) so it can't go wrong. [reasoned]

P7. Most words carry no evidence. For the 6 / 7 decision a feature counts only if a 7 writes it much more often than a

  1. Carrying nothing: words printed in the prompt, prep-book openers (De nos jours, Il est indéniable que, Force est de constater), je pense que, parce que, plain subject-verb-object sentences, letterheads. Subtracting: basic errors, repeated words, a linear list of facts. Every word that carries no evidence is not neutral: it is another gender, agreement and spelling choice that can go wrong. [reasoned from F3, F6]

P8. Spend the first sentence on the strongest evidence. The first sentence you write gets the most weight. It should answer the reader's open question (A: whatever the printed start leaves hanging; B: your position) in one correct complex sentence with a precise word that is not in the prompt. [reasoned from F6]

P9. Let the genre carry its own structures. They cost nothing extra and also prove "conventions of the genre" and "respect of the situation".

  • A news story: passé composé for events against imparfait for background, plus-que-parfait for what had happened before, the passive, attribution (selon les enquêteurs), the journalistic conditional for unconfirmed facts (le coffre aurait appartenu à…), and a quote in « » with the speech tag after it (« … », a confié le retraité).

  • An opinion letter: Madame, Monsieur,; a one-clause reference to the statement in your own words; concession (certes… mais, même si); the subjunctive after il faut / il est essentiel / il est grand temps que; the conditional for proposals and hypotheses; nominal phrases (la prise de conscience, l'usage excessif). Don't force a subjunctive into a news story: it costs words and reads as a template. [reasoned]

P10. Pack across criteria, cap the risk. One sentence can prove a task move, a structure, two precise words and a link at once; that stacking is free. Risky structures are not: at most two medium- or high-risk structures per sentence; sentences of about 12 to 25 French words, with one short one per paragraph (mixing simple and complex sentences is itself evidence). [reasoned: the chance a sentence is clean is the product of each structure's chance of being right]

P11. Every criterion gets two carriers, at least one low-risk. Graders agree exactly less than half the time; one proof can be skimmed, discounted or come out wrong. Two carriers for each range item, three different kinds of link. [reasoned from F3]

P12. Relations, not sequences. Mark at least three different logical relations (cause, consequence, purpose, concession or opposition, condition), not counting time markers (puis, ensuite, d'abord). A bare "however" proves B1. In B the concession must weigh something ("admittedly X, but Y matters more"), not just turn. [reasoned from F5]

P13. Develop, nuance, make precise. The publisher's own triad. The box contains at least one precise detail (a number, an object with a property, a named role, a place, a time), one idea developed with a reason and an example or consequence, and one nuance (a concession, a limit, an uncertainty). [reasoned from F2]

P14. Precise words that aren't in the prompt. At least five precise words not printed in the prompt, plus one idiom or fixed collocation; at least two nouns that refer back instead of repeating (the dog becomes l'animal, Mehdi becomes le retraité; the prompt's own septuagénaire is copying); no content word repeated; each prompt keyword used at most once, and reformulated rather than copied (addiction aux réseaux sociaux becomes dépendance aux écrans). [reasoned from F3, F5]

P15. Tie every proof to this prompt. The box will be prepared in advance, so the risk that it reads as a template is real. Every complex sentence carries this prompt's people, objects or claim; no free-standing set phrase longer than about four words, except the letter conventions. [reasoned from F6]

P16. Pitch the box at a level you can match. If the box reads like C1 and the rest like B1, the grader's best explanation is a memorised opening, and the box is discounted after the fact. Aim at solid B2 with two or three upper-B2 features, the top of what you can also write without the script, and keep the rest at the same register and tenses. [reasoned]

P17. The box must outweigh an ordinary rest (dilution). The grader's level is roughly a blend of box and rest, with early text weighted up. With a plain but correct rest, the box should be about 45% or more of the words you will actually submit; about 35 to 40% is enough only if your plain French is reliably clean. Writing far past the minimum after the point dilutes the box. [reasoned: a planning number, not a measurement]

P18. Put the skill up front; leave the rest only compliance. Everything that needs skill goes in the box. What is left must be doable in plain, clean French: reaching the minimum, finishing, the closing formula. Like a chess opening that wins material: the conversion needs no brilliant moves, only no blunders. [reasoned]

P19. Each section must reach the bar alone. Task A is proven only in A and task B only in B; the language criteria may pool across both texts, but nobody knows how, and any A box may meet any B box on exam day. Each box locks its own section. [reasoned from F2]

C. "The point", defined

The point is the end of the shortest opening of the text after which all of these hold, for a strict grader:

  1. Gates closed in the box.

    • A: continues straight on from the printed start (no restating), third-person news voice, at least one paragraph break inside the box, and the content the printed start promises is delivered (see the A rule below).

    • B: Madame, Monsieur,; a one-clause reference to the statement in your own words; a clear position; all three arguments present, each with its reason; one concession that weighs something.

  2. Range proven, with two carriers where it counts.

    • A: passé composé and imparfait in correct contrast, plus anteriority (plus-que-parfait, venir de, or après avoir + participle); a genre mood (the journalistic conditional) or a quote with the speech tag after it, ideally both; at least two complex devices from dont / où, gérondif, participial clause, past infinitive, passive.

    • B: at least one visible subjunctive; at least one conditional; at least two complex devices.

    • Both: at least three kinds of logical relation (P12); about five precise words not in the prompt plus one idiom or collocation; at least two reference-back nouns; at least one visible feminine or plural agreement.

  3. Coverage. One precise detail, one developed idea, one nuance (P13). In B, at least one argument fully developed (claim, concrete detail, consequence or remedy).

  4. Control is the default. No error in the box, and enough clean sentences that a later slip reads as occasional: about four or five in A, about six to eight in B.

  5. Dilution. The box is at least about 45% of the total you plan to submit (P17).

The Section A rule on the ending. The lenses disagreed: one wanted the whole arc, outcome included, in the box (about 70 to 85 words); two let an ordinary ending sit in the rest (about 50 to 70 words). The merged rule: the box must contain whatever the printed start sets up as the story's content. If the start promises a discovery, the discovery; an "original solution", the solution; a reaction "that caught the media's interest", the reaction; "recognised by a guard…", what followed. A plain closing line (who got the object back, what happened next) can then sit in the rest, because writing it needs no skill. If the prompt's interest lies in the outcome itself, the outcome is in the box.

The Section B rule on the arguments. One lens proposed announcing the three arguments as a list of nouns and developing only the first (about 80 words). The merge keeps a stricter rule: a noun list proves the count but not "développez", and two bare arguments in a plain rest read as a B1 list for two thirds of the task, which is the heaviest criterion. So each argument has its reason inside the box, one is fully developed, and the noun-list plan sentence stays available as a cheap way to show organisation.

If an element can be removed and everything above still holds, it was dead weight and goes.

D. "Disaster", defined

After the point, only conspicuous trouble pulls the score below 7: a gate that fails, a pattern of errors, or something that makes the grader reread the box as memorised.

Disaster What it looks like in the text
D1 Too short the final text under 80 (A) or 200 (B) of your own words; copied prompt words don't count
D2 Missing or broken task part A: no ending, the story abandoned, the printed start contradicted (not the dog, not the lake); B: an argument missing or a bare claim with no reason, no conclusion, the position reversed without framing it as a concession, a paragraph off topic
D3 The same basic error, again and again the same error type three or more times (noun gender, subject-verb agreement, être/avoir in the passé composé, a/à, et/est, ces/ses, -é/-er), or errors in most sentences of a paragraph
D4 A sentence nobody can decode any sentence a French reader can't work out on the first read
D5 Tense collapse in A switching between present and past with no logic
D6 Register or genre break tu to the newspaper, slang, SMS spelling, English words; in A, narrating as je outside quotes
D7 The script shows a sharp drop in level right after the box, generic template paragraphs that don't fit the prompt, padding that repeats the box or the prompt
D8 Unfinished a text that stops mid-sentence

Not a disaster: plain, simple, correct sentences; basic connectors (et, mais, puis, donc, ensuite, enfin); a modest, predictable ending or conclusion; arguments two and three thinner than the first, as long as each keeps its reason; a few isolated slips of different types; wording that sounds a little like English (CLB 7 allows it); a length just over the minimum.

What the rest should look like. Plain simple and compound sentences; the same register, person and tenses; reuse the box's structures and add no new hard ones; not a stream of four-word sentences either (a sharp drop in style gives the box away); the minimum plus about 10 to 20%; a clean ending (A: one or two sentences of outcome; B: a short conclusion that is specific to the topic, then Veuillez agréer, Madame, Monsieur, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées.).

What this assumes about you. The box supplies the controlled complex sentences, the precise words and the developed, nuanced content a 6 lacks. It can't rescue a rest full of basic errors: after the point you need to write like a clean NCLC 6, plain and correct.

Leave out of the box: the passé simple (news reports use the passé composé); the past subjunctive, ne explétif, après que, quoique, auquel / duquel; reflexive past-participle traps (se sont rendu compte); past-participle agreement with avoir and a preceding object (high signal, high risk); adjectives that agree with je (convaincu(e); prefer je partage entièrement ce constat); nouns whose gender you aren't sure of.

Carried over from the speaking pilot (these are about English turning into French, so they hold in writing too): never "I know what..." or "tell him what..." in an indirect question (it invites qu'est-ce que; write "whether / how / when" or reword); the English must produce the intended French on its own, without a note overruling it; a link counts only if it joins clauses or sentences (a topic marker or a preposition before a noun is not a link); a precise word counts only if the English produces it and the prompt doesn't print it.

F. What we are least sure of

  1. How the score is formed. The criterion weights, how A and B combine, whether one grader reads both texts, and how the five marks map to levels are not public.

  2. Where exactly the 6/7 cut sits inside B2. The tables agree that "just B2" is not enough; the margin rule (upper B2) rests on a noise model fitted to published agreement rates.

  3. The penalty under the minimum. No official TEF rule was found; the TCF, a different test, can rate such a text "A1 not reached". How the typed test counts words (is l'animal one word?) is unknown, so aim at the minimum plus 10 to 15%.

  4. The dilution share (about 45%), the error-rate thresholds in D3 and the risk numbers in P10 are reasoned planning figures, not measurements.

  5. Template detection and halo come from research on other tests and on teachers, not on trained TEF graders.

The standards every box was held to

These came out of the four red-team reviews (W-A1, W-A2, W-B1, W-B2). They apply to all 12 boxes so the boxes are held to one standard.

  1. The English makes its own French. Count only the evidence the English produces on its own. A translator note may choose between natural renderings; it never overrules the English. If a Why claim depends on a word or structure the English doesn't produce (l'agent de sécurité from "guard", à condition qu'on ait from "provided that one has"), change the English or drop the claim.

  2. Two complex devices from the list are enough (at least one low-risk). A third goes unless it also carries something else the conditions need.

  3. Two reference-back nouns are enough. A spare one that a pronoun replaces cleanly goes.

  4. Precise details. A detail goes only if another detail in the same stretch already gives that precision and the word carries no structure ("on Sunday", "iron"). Details that are the only carrier of a number, an age or a place stay.

  5. The risk cap is checked sentence by sentence. At most two medium- or high-risk structures per sentence, and every Why claim about it must be true.

  6. Journalistic conditional. The bare conditional is enough when it can only read as unconfirmed information. Add a short source ("according to the police") only when the bare form could read as a wish or another meaning.

  7. Idiom or collocation: either one meets the condition.

  8. Purpose link: pour + infinitive counts; don't spend more words on it.
  9. Three kinds of logical relation are enough. A fourth goes unless it carries a needed structure.

  10. Repetition, counted by root. No content word repeated, and a word and its derived forms count as one (calmé / calme, beau / beauté, livre / livres). Each prompt keyword, with its derived forms, appears at most once.

  11. Visible agreement: one feminine or one plural agreement is enough.

  12. Control floor. A Section A box keeps at least four sentences; a Section B box at least six. Cuts never take a box below that.

  13. One writing pace. Minutes into the section = French words in the box ÷ 9.5 (typing a practised text, with one reread), rounded. Restate every Point on this basis.

  14. Section B subjunctives. Two carriers, both produced by the English without a note, at least one not a fixed phrase (quel que soit counts as a fixed phrase).

  15. No stock content phrases. A cliché ("this scourge knows no borders") can't stand in for an argument's reason. Letter conventions (the salutation, "I read in your newspaper that...") are exempt.

  16. Salutation in English: "Madam, Sir," (it gives Madame, Monsieur,; "Dear Sir or Madam" invites Cher Monsieur ou Madame).

  17. A scanning grader must be able to count three arguments. Where a paragraph break costs no words, give each argument its own paragraph.

  18. The rest plan adds up. The reserve in Working lists the rest of the text sentence by sentence, with rough French word counts that bring the whole text to the minimum plus about 10% (Section A at least about 90 French words, Section B at least about 220), with new content only (no echoes of the box). The Why's stated total must match it.

  19. Intensifiers ("fully", "very", "real", "simply") are dead weight unless they carry a structure.

  20. Complex devices in Section B (from the final audit). They count from this list: a relative clause with dont, où or ce qui; a gérondif; a past infinitive; a participial clause; a passive; si + imperfect → conditional. The subjunctive and a plain conditional count under moods, not here. A Section B box needs two, at least one low-risk. If it has fewer, swap wording inside an existing sentence to add one; don't add a sentence.
Lens 1 · What is measured and how the score is formed

Lens 1: the instrument. What TEF Canada Expression écrite measures, and how the score is formed

Foundation file for the NCLC 7 floor writing pilot, written 2026-11-22. Every factual claim carries a tag: [source: Sn] (the numbered list at the end gives the URL or document) or [reasoned] (my inference, with the step shown). Descriptor wording from the frameworks is paraphrased in English, not copied.

0. The answer in brief

  1. The cut is not the B1/B2 border. It sits inside B2. On today's 699-point scale, NCLC 7 in writing is 428 to 471, and B2 runs from 400 to 499. A text that graders call "just B2" (400 to 427) is still NCLC 6. So "not below 7" means "a solid B2", and Min's own phrase "B2+" turns out to be the right target, not just a loose one.

  2. Two graders score independently on an analytic grid with five criteria: the task in A, the task in B, syntax, lexicon, and coherence/cohesion. Each criterion is marked 0 to 10. Fixed weights turn the marks into one score for both sections together, and the two task criteria weigh the most.

  3. The graders are noisy. They agree on the exact CEFR level only 45% of the time. If you aim only at the middle of NCLC 7, noise leaves you below 7 about 3 times in 10. If you aim at the top of NCLC 7 or the bottom of NCLC 8 (upper B2), noise still keeps you at 7 or above about 9 times in 10. The box has to show upper-B2 evidence.

  4. What separates a 6 from a 7: relations instead of lists; complex sentences that are correct, not just attempted; precise words instead of the prompt's own words; information that is precise and detailed; and ideas that are developed, nuanced and made specific.

  5. What can be proven early: anything that only needs to exist once (a structure, a word, a connector, a detail). What needs the whole text: error rates (can only be protected, never banked) and the task's shape (A must reach an ending; B must have three arguments).

  6. Test: the EA-019 box is about 70 to 85 English words, which means the point in Section A falls at about the minimum length: there is no shortcut. The EB-014 box is about 120 to 140 English words, roughly 60% of a 220-word answer.

1. The two tasks, as set

Format [source: S1, S2, S9]. The test lasts one hour in two time-limited sections, and it is computer-based: it is typed in the publisher's Paragraphe software, with paper used only for candidates who cannot use a computer [source: S10]. A virtual keyboard supplies accented characters [source: S2]. Candidates may ask for scrap paper for the writing test, which is taken back at the end. No dictionary is allowed [source: S9, S10]. A candidate who ends Section A early cannot go back to it [source: S9].

Section A, 25 minutes [source: S1, S2, S9]. The candidate sees the start of a news item (the faits divers column) and must finish the article: add a continuation of at least 80 words, written in several paragraphs. The handbook says this task assesses the ability to pass on information [source: S9]. The publisher's own advice for A [source: S11]: do not copy the start (it earns nothing and costs time), do not summarise or rephrase it, bring in new information, and use the headline for ideas.

Section B, 35 minutes [source: S1, S2, S9]. The candidate reads a statement presented as taken from a newspaper and must write a letter to the newspaper saying what they think of it, at least 200 words, and: "Développez au moins 3 arguments pour défendre votre point de vue." [source: S1]. The handbook says this task assesses the ability to express and justify a point of view [source: S9]. The publisher's advice for B [source: S12]: the letter form is not compulsory; a formal letterhead earns no points; open with a short sentence that reacts to the statement; generic introductions and conclusions that would fit any topic are ignored by graders; spend the words on your own arguments.

Check against FRAME.md. The timings, minimums, paragraphs, three arguments and the letter set-up are all confirmed. Two corrections:

  • The score on the certificate is out of 699, not 450 [source: S3]. The 0 to 450 figures are the "old score equivalence" column, which IRCC requires for Express Entry [source: S3, S6]. FRAME's bands (NCLC 6 271-309, NCLC 7 310-348, NCLC 8 349-370) are correct for that column [source: S6].

  • "Letter" is looser than FRAME implies: the letter form is optional and earns nothing in itself [source: S12].

2. How a score is produced

2.1 Who scores. Every script is scored by two graders working independently, both trained specialists in language teaching, on a standardised grid [source: S1, S3, S9]. The marking is done centrally by the publisher (CCI Paris Île-de-France), not at the test centre [source: S9]. When the two marks differ strongly, an arbitration step sets the final mark [source: S8]. A re-mark cannot be requested [source: S3].

2.2 The grid. The publisher's own researchers describe it [source: S8]. It is an analytic grid with descriptive scales and five criteria:

  • two pragmatic criteria, one for each task: how well the candidate carried out task A, and task B;
  • three linguistic criteria: syntax, lexicon, coherence/cohesion.

Each criterion is marked 0 to 10. The marks are combined with fixed weights into one score for the test, and the score scale is cut into seven bands (<A1, then A1 to C2) [source: S8]. The same authors say the pragmatic criteria carry the preponderant weight in the score [source: S8]. The weights themselves are not published.

2.3 What the grid looks at, in the publisher's public words. The candidate document [source: S1] lists two groups.

  • Quality and effectiveness of the texts: respecting the situation, the overall organisation, the clarity of the information or the argument, and the ability to develop, nuance and make one's points precise.

  • Range and control of written French: lexicon, syntax (sentences, tenses and moods), spelling.

It also prints the questions graders ask, each with a scale of answers [source: S1] (paraphrased):

  • Is the text clear and limpid, or confused and incoherent?
  • Is the information sketchy, vague, precise or detailed?
  • Are the sentences simple, complex, controlled?
  • Are tenses and moods used correctly?
  • Is ordinary spelling under control?
  • Is the vocabulary correct, precise and appropriate?

The 2021 blog posts give the same list for each section: relevance of the information to the subject, quality of the information (examples, details, illustrations), overall coherence, quality of sentences and vocabulary (variety, precision), syntax and punctuation [source: S11, S12].

2.4 From marks to a number. The marks are "converted into a calibrated score" [source: S3]. The method is not published. The publisher's researchers work with many-facet Rasch models and rater-severity corrections [source: S17, S8], so calibration plausibly adjusts for harsh or lenient graders [reasoned].

2.5 One number for both sections. There is one writing score per candidate, built from the five criteria [source: S3, S8]. The two task criteria are each tied to one section. The three linguistic criteria are listed without reference to a section, so they are most likely judged on the two texts together [reasoned from S8; the grid itself is not public].

2.6 How graders actually behave (the publisher's own 2021 data, about 35,000 scripts) [source: S8]:

  • The five criterion marks correlate very strongly: 0.88 to 0.95. One factor explains 92% of their variance. In practice graders form one global level and the five criteria follow it.

  • The two graders' scores correlate at 0.74. They give the same CEFR level 45% of the time, and levels at most one apart 92% of the time. On single criteria, exact agreement is only 25 to 27%.

  • An automated model the publisher built recovers the graders' level 76% of the time. The features it relies on most are lexical: how typical the words are of each CEFR level, the share of words spelled correctly (words found in the dictionary), and the number of different words. Next come sequences of word classes, which capture syntax [source: S8].

  • The population is centred on B: 8% A1/A2, 59% B1/B2, 33% C1/C2 [source: S8].

5.5 Synthesis: the five discriminators between a 6 and a 7 [reasoned, from 5.1 to 5.4]:

  • D1. Relations, not lists. B1 orders things (first, then, finally; "however"). B2 shows why and how ideas connect: cause, consequence, purpose, concession, evaluation. Chronological markers and a bare "however" prove B1, not B2.

  • D2. Complex sentences that are correct, not attempted. NCLC 6 attempts complex structures and misses them. NCLC 7 gets them right. A wrong complex sentence is evidence for 6.

  • D3. Precise and varied words. These are words that name the thing exactly and do not recycle the prompt's words. Repeated content words are a B1 symptom.

  • D4. Information that is precise and detailed, ideas developed with support, nuanced, and made specific.

  • D5. Control as a rate. Errors are occasional and not systematic, and none blocks the meaning. This is judged over the whole text.

6. Principles

P1. One score, two task proofs. The score is one weighted sum over both texts, but each task criterion belongs to one section [source: S8]. Rule: each section's box must settle its own task criterion by itself. Linguistic evidence can pool across A and B, so each box must still carry its own linguistic proofs (see P12) [reasoned].

P2. The cut is mid-B2. NCLC 7 starts at 428 on the 699 scale, 28 points into B2 [source: S4, S5]. Rule: a borderline B2 is a 6. The box must show B2 features that are clearly controlled, never merely attempted [reasoned].

P3. Aim one band up. With two-grader noise of about 39 points (section 4), the script must deserve about 470 to 490 to stay at 7 or above roughly 9 times in 10 [reasoned]. Rule: build the box to the B2+ descriptors (rare, non-systematic slips; a variety of linking words; an argument developed systematically), not to the B2 minimum.

P4. The task decides first. The task criteria carry the preponderant weight [source: S8]. Rule: the box's first job is to prove the task (A: a detailed continuation that reaches an outcome; B: a clear stance plus three developed arguments). Its second job is the linguistic proofs. Linguistic brilliance cannot rescue a failed task [reasoned].

P5. One global level, so no weak link. The criteria move together (one factor, 92%) [source: S8]. Rule: a visibly weak criterion drags the global level down, and the global level then sets all five marks. The weaknesses graders see most easily are spelling errors and repeated words. Those are also the features that best predict graders' levels (S8), and they are the cheapest to fix on a reread. The box should have zero spelling errors and no repeated content word [reasoned].

P6. Three kinds of evidence, three timetables [reasoned]:

  • Existence evidence (range: a structure, a precise word, a varied connector, a detail, a concession). One clear instance proves it, so it can be completed early and banked.

  • Rate evidence (control: errors per stretch of text, how systematic they are). This is a frequency, so it can never be banked. The box can only build a clean prior, and the rest must not create a pattern.

  • Structure evidence (task shape: an ending in A; three arguments in B; paragraphs; minimum length). This depends on where the text goes. In A, the box can contain the whole shape. In B, the three-argument rule pushes the point late.

P7. Relations beat sequences (D1). Rule: each box must mark at least three different logical relations (cause, consequence, purpose, concession or opposition, condition), on top of any time markers. In B, the concession must evaluate something ("even if X, it is essential that Y"), not just say "however" [reasoned from S15].

P8. The TEF triad: develop, nuance, make precise [source: S1]. Rule: each box contains at least one precise detail (a number, an object with a property, a place, a time, a named role), one idea developed with a reason and an example or consequence, and one nuance (a concession, a qualification, a limit) [reasoned].

P9. Only write complex sentences you can get right (D2). A complex sentence with an error moves the grader toward 6 [source: S13]. Rule: at least two error-free complex sentences of different types in each box (for example a relative clause plus a tense contrast in A; a subordinate clause plus a subjunctive or conditional in B). They must carry the prompt's content, so they cannot be read as memorised chunks [reasoned].

P10. Generic text is worth zero. Copying the prompt, letterheads, and all-purpose introductions or conclusions earn nothing [source: S11, S12]. Rule: the box starts with content in its first sentence. In B the opening is one short sentence that reacts to the statement in your own words [reasoned].

P11. Minimums are task rules, not quality. Missing them almost certainly costs the task criterion, which weighs most. The TEF's exact rule is not published; the TCF (a different publisher) says a script that misses the word count may be rated "A1 not reached" [source: S18]. Graders' marks also track length in general [source: S19]. Rule: the box may be shorter than the minimum, but the full text must clear 80 and 200 words with a margin of about 10 to 15%. How the software counts words (for example, elided forms like l'enquête) is unknown [reasoned].

P12. Say the key things twice. Graders disagree more often than they agree (45% exact) [source: S8]. Rule: each kind of key proof (complex sentence, precise word, logical connector) appears at least twice in the box, so one skimming or one discounted instance does not erase it [reasoned].

P13. The genre is part of the task. B2 narration follows the genre's conventions, and NCLC 7 adapts to the reader [source: S15, S13]. Rule: A is written as a news report (third person, past tenses, factual, specific, a quoted witness or official). B is addressed to the newspaper (formal "vous" register, a reference to the article, the stance stated) [reasoned].

8. Early versus whole-text: where "the point" can sit

Section 6 gave the three kinds of evidence (P6). Applied to the grid [reasoned]:

  • Syntax, lexicon and coherence as range: existence evidence. They can be fully proven early, in the first 40 to 60 words of either section, if the sentences are packed (Lens 3's job).

  • Syntax, lexicon and spelling as control: rate evidence. They are never fully proven early. The box sets the grader's starting estimate ("this writer controls complex French"), and the rest can only spoil it. The point for control is reached when the box holds enough clean complex text that a few later slips still read as "occasional". As a rough working rule (not from a source), a slip rate of about one per 40 to 50 words, with no error type repeated three times and no sentence whose meaning is unclear, still reads as B2.

  • Task A: structure evidence that can sit entirely in the box, because the whole arc (discovery, reaction, outcome) fits in 70 to 85 words. The task verb is "finish". Leaving the outcome to "the rest" puts the heaviest criterion at risk. So in A the point sits at about the end of the minimal complete story, close to the 80-word minimum.

  • Task B: structure evidence that needs three developed arguments. In B the point cannot come before the third argument has its support and the concession is written: about 110 to 140 words, 55 to 70% of a 200-word text. A shorter box (stance plus three bare claims) leaves "developed" to the rest, and a thin rest would then read as B1 listing.

So, from the instrument's side, "the point" is reached when four things are all true: (a) every existence proof is in; (b) the section's task shape is complete (A: an outcome; B: three supported arguments and a concession); (c) the box so far is error-free; (d) the evidence is pitched at upper B2 (P3).

9. "Disaster" in instrument terms, and the minimum word counts

A disaster is anything after the point that could move the weighted total down by about half a CEFR band (around 50 points), which is the margin P3 builds in [reasoned]. Ranked by likely size [reasoned from S8's weighting and the descriptors in section 5]:

  1. Task failure in either section: under the minimum (S18 shows how harsh a sister test is); in A, an article left unfinished, a contradiction of the given opening, or going back to summarise it; in B, fewer than three real arguments, a stance that flips, or leaving the statement's topic. These hit the heaviest criteria.

  2. A systematic error pattern in the rest: the same error type again and again (agreement, auxiliary, tense mixing, verb endings), or any sentence whose meaning is unclear. This turns "occasional slips" (B2) into "noticeable errors" (B1). Because the criteria move together, the drop spreads to all five marks.

  3. Breakdown of coherence: contradictions, unrelated jumps, sentences cut off when time runs out.

  4. Register collapse: texting style, English words, a first-person chat in A.

Not a disaster [reasoned from S13, S14, S15]: plain, simple, correct sentences; a modest conclusion; a few unrelated slips; wording that sounds a little like English (CLB 7 allows it); repeated simple connectors in the rest.

Minimums. The box can be shorter than 80 or 200 words. The rest must (i) bring the section past its minimum with a margin of about 10 to 15%, (ii) in A, keep the paragraph breaks, and (iii) in B, add whatever development the arguments still need, plus a short conclusion that is specific to the topic. Every extra sentence is also another chance to make an error, so the rest should be plain and short rather than ambitious [reasoned].

10. Both sections, one score

  • A disaster in either section's task criterion can sink the whole score whatever the other section does, because that criterion is heavily weighted [reasoned from S8].

  • Linguistic evidence probably pools across the two texts (2.5). So a clean, ambitious Section A text helps the linguistic marks that B also feeds, and the reverse [reasoned]. The range of tenses and moods is only fully visible across both: A shows the past-tense system, B shows the subjunctive and conditional.

  • Min does not know which A prompt will be paired with which B prompt. So each box must be self-sufficient: its own task proof, plus a full set of linguistic proofs. Doubling the linguistic proofs across sections costs almost nothing, because they ride inside content sentences, and it also covers P12 [reasoned].

  • Section A comes first and cannot be revisited [source: S9]. The grader probably reads it first too, so A also sets the first impression (Lens 2's territory).

11. Making it writable in French (what the instrument rewards versus what a learner can produce)

The grid rewards the structure (a correct complex sentence, a correct subjunctive in a natural trigger), not how rare the form is [reasoned from S1, S13]. That opens some cheap routes [reasoned]:

  • Subjunctive at low risk: with regular -er verbs, the subjunctive after il faut que or il est essentiel que looks the same as the present for je, tu, il and ils ("que les parents limitent"). The grader sees a correct subjunctive structure, and the learner cannot get the form wrong.

  • A past-tense contrast with no rare forms: the imparfait for background, the passé composé for events, and the plus-que-parfait for what had happened before. In A this comes naturally from the prompt's own frame ("promenait… quand… a fait").

  • Relatives with qui, que, où are high yield and low risk. Dont and lequel forms, and past participles that agree with a preceding direct object (la boîte qu'il avait trouvée), are where learners slip. Use them only if drilled.

  • Imparfait with depuis ("qui cherchait cette boîte depuis quarante ans") is a compact B2 marker in a narrative.

  • Precise words: prefer common-but-exact words a learner can spell (enquête, commissariat, propriétaire, notification, sommeil) over rare ones.

Lens 3 owns the packing and risk trade-off. From the instrument's side, the rule is simply P9: every complex form in the box must be one Min can write correctly under time pressure, after a reread.

12. What I am least sure of

  1. The weights. The task criteria are "preponderant" [S8], but the numbers are unpublished. The 50-point idea of a disaster in section 9 assumes a task criterion carries roughly a quarter to a third of the score.

  2. Whether the linguistic criteria are per section or pooled. S8 lists five criteria for the whole test, which suggests pooling, but the grid itself is not public.

  3. The rule below the minimum. No official TEF statement was found. Third-party "automatic penalty" claims were unverified, and the TCF's rule belongs to another test.

  4. The noise model. It fits the published agreement rates well, but it assumes that the final score is the mean of the two graders, and that calibration and arbitration do not cut much noise. If they do, the margin needed falls (the SD-30 column).

  5. How the 0 to 10 criterion marks map to CEFR levels. Eleven steps against seven bands suggests sub-levels inside B2, which would matter for a cut in the middle of B2, but this is not documented.

  6. The CEFR text. The Council of Europe server refused automated download. I read the 2018 Companion Volume from a text copy and paraphrased the scales; their B2/B2+ split follows the CEFR 2001 layout as I recall it.

  7. The error-rate rule in section 8 (about one slip per 40 to 50 words, no type three times) is a working heuristic, not a published threshold.

EB-014 (Section B). "Social media addiction: adults too!"

Required elements, from this lens

  • Opening (situation plus stance): salutation and one sentence that restates the statement in your own words and takes a clear position. No generic shell.

  • Argument 1 (mechanism plus concrete example): why adults get hooked (apps built to hold attention; notifications), with a specific scene (a meal, a meeting).

  • Argument 2 (consequence, with "because" or a relative): harm to family or social life (parents on screens, children who copy them).

  • Argument 3 (cause and effect, fact versus opinion): health (screens delay sleep; comparing yourself with others breeds anxiety).

  • Evaluated concession plus a mood: grant one benefit (keeping in touch with distant family), then weigh it with a subjunctive or conditional proposal ("it is essential that adults learn to disconnect, for example by…").

  • Across the box: at least 4 logical relation types; "first / then / finally" may frame the three arguments, but they do not count as B2 proof; at least 2 correct complex sentences; 5 or more precise words not in the prompt (platforms, notifications, sleep, anxious, disconnect); formal register; zero spelling or agreement errors.

Lens 2 · How the grader’s verdict forms and when it locks

Lens 2: judge.md (how the grader's verdict forms and when it locks)

Foundation derivation for the NCLC 7 floor writing pilot, 2026-11-22. Every factual claim is labelled [source: ...] or [reasoned]. Full source list at the end. "You" and "Min" mean the candidate.

0. The question, through this lens

A TEF writing grader does not "add up" a text. A grader reads, forms a belief about the writer's level, and keeps or revises that belief as the text goes on. The question "what is the least Min can write, after which only a disaster pulls the score below NCLC 7?" becomes a question about that belief:

At what word does the grader's belief "this writer is at NCLC 7 or above" become so well supported that nothing an ordinary (non-disastrous) continuation could contain would overturn it?

To answer it, this file sets out (1) who the TEF graders are and what they are told to look at, (2) what research shows about how writing raters actually read, (3) what separates a 6 from a 7 as seen from the grader's chair, (4) a decision model with numbered principles, (5) a concrete definition of "disaster", (6) where the point can sit, (7) whether Section A helps Section B, and (8) a test on EA-019 and EB-014.

1. Facts about who judges a TEF Canada writing test

1.1 Two independent correctors, trained, central. The TEF writing test "est évaluée par deux correcteurs, de façon indépendante, qui sont des spécialistes de la didactique des langues" [source: Le français des affaires, Exemples de sujets TEF – Expression écrite, p. 3]. The 2018 e-TEF candidate manual says "au moins deux correcteurs" and that the writing test is corrected by Le français des affaires itself, not by the local centre [source: Manuel du candidat e-TEF, CCI Paris Île-de-France, janvier 2018, pp. 12 and annex]. The 2026 centre manager's booklet repeats that writing and speaking are "évaluées par deux correcteurs/évaluateurs différents", that TEF Canada writing is 2 subjects in 60 minutes, and that writing is taken on computer [source: Livret du responsable TEF 2026, pp. 5, 9].

1.2 What the correctors are told to judge. The publisher's own list [source: Exemples de sujets TEF – EE, p. 3]:

  • La qualité et l'efficacité des textes: le respect de la situation; l'organisation générale du texte; la clarté de l'information ou de l'argumentation; la capacité à développer, nuancer, préciser ses propos.

  • La richesse et la maîtrise de la langue écrite: le lexique; la syntaxe (phrases, temps et modes…); l'orthographe.

  • Guiding questions printed beside the criteria: "Le texte est-il clair, limpide, confus, incohérent ?", "Les informations sont-elles sommaires, vagues, précises, détaillées ?", "Les phrases sont-elles simples, complexes, maîtrisées ?", "Les temps et modes sont-ils correctement utilisés ?", "L'orthographe usuelle est-elle maîtrisée ?", "Le lexique employé est-il juste, précis, adéquat ?"

  • The grid gives "un niveau de performance globale sur l'échelle du CECR".

Read as a judge's checklist, these questions are short ordinal scales. "Sommaires → vagues → précises → détaillées" and "simples → complexes → maîtrisées" are ladders, and a grader places the text on a rung of each [reasoned].

1.3 The graders are themselves measured. Researchers at the CCI Paris Île-de-France have published generalizability-theory and many-facet Rasch analyses of TEF writing scores, including rater severity profiles at each cut-point, and conclude that modelling severity at the cut-points improves reliability while explaining only a limited share of error variance [source: Casanova & Demeuse 2011, Mesure et évaluation en éducation 34(1); Casanova & Demeuse 2016, MEE 39(3); abstracts only, full texts blocked]. So the final score probably comes from two independent grids plus a statistical model, not from one person's mood [reasoned]. Severity differences still exist and are only partly corrected, so a box must work for a strict corrector, not just an average one [reasoned].

1.4 Where NCLC 7 sits on the scale (FRAME.md check).

  • IRCC tells Express Entry candidates to enter the certificate's "Équivalence ancien score" column, where writing NCLC 6 = 271–309, NCLC 7 = 310–348, NCLC 8 = 349–370; the "/699" score "is not compatible" with the profile [source: IRCC, Express Entry language testing page]. FRAME.md's numbers match this column.

  • The certificate's main score is out of 699. The publisher's table for tests from 11 December 2023 gives writing NCLC 6 = 379–427, NCLC 7 = 428–471, NCLC 8 = 472–511. Its 2019–2023 column gave NCLC 7 = 450–499 for every test [source: Le français des affaires, Correspondance score TEF – niveau NCLC]. IRCC notes a temporary change of NCLC levels between 11 Dec 2023 and 6 May 2024 that was reverted [source: IRCC page above]. Which /699 range is current for writing is Lens 1's job to settle. This lens only needs what follows.

  • On the /699 scale, B1 = 300–399 and B2 = 400–499 [source: France Compétences TEF fiche and TEF IRN results page, as reported in search results; not opened directly].

  • Consequence: NCLC 7 in writing is not the floor of B2. It starts about 28 points (current table) or 50 points (2019–2023 table) into the 100-point B2 band. A text a grader calls "just B2" can still be NCLC 6. FRAME.md's phrase "NCLC 7 / B2+" overstates it: NCLC 7 is mid-B2. NCLC 8 starts at upper B2. Min needs a text that reads as clearly B2, with a few upper-B2 features as margin, not a borderline B1/B2 text [reasoned from the two tables].

1.5 What is not public (as far as I could find). The grid's weights; how Section A and Section B combine into one score; whether the same corrector reads both of a candidate's texts; how a disagreement between the two correctors is settled; and what the TEF does with a text under the minimum word count. For the last point, the sister test TCF (France Éducation international) says a production can be scored "A1 non atteint" if a task does not reach the word count or is off topic [source: FEI, TCF sample EE document, as quoted in search results; page blocked to automated access]. The TEF rule may differ.

2. What research says about how writing raters read

R1. Raters scan before they read. The prototypical sequence in think-aloud studies of experienced ESL raters is: "Scan the composition for surface-level identification, such as length, format, paragraphing", then read while classifying errors and interpreting rhetoric, then articulate a score. Some decisions come "within a minute or so" [source: Cumming, Kantor & Powers, ETS RM-01-04, pp. 21–22; published as Modern Language Journal 86(1), 2002]. So layout and visible length set a prior before a single sentence is read [reasoned].

R2. The first intuitive impression steers the verdict. In a large-scale immigration writing test, raters had to reconcile their intuitive impression of the text, its specific features and the scale wording; the initial intuitive impression strongly shaped decisions, and the scale was often used to articulate it [source: Lumley 2002, Language Testing 19(3), abstract via ACER record].

R3. Raters predict. Examiners comment on "the likely characteristics of the candidate" and predict "further performance" mid-script ("this is not going to be a better paper is it?"); overall evaluations "relate back to earlier thoughts" [source: Crisp 2007, Research Matters 4, Cambridge Assessment].

R4. But good raters read to the end. Proficient essay scorers tend to read the whole essay before judging it; less proficient ones break off to compare with the rubric [source: Wolfe, Kao & Ranney 1998, Written Communication 15(4), abstract]. TEF correctors are trained specialists, so assume they read every word [reasoned]. The "point" is therefore not where reading stops. It is where the verdict stops moving.

R5. Attention shifts with quality. Raters gave more attention to rhetoric and ideas in essays they rated very high (30.0% of decisions vs 22.5% for very low essays, ESL raters), and native-composition raters gave relatively more attention to language in low essays (32.3% vs 26.0%) [source: RM-01-04, pp. 59–60]. Once a text looks linguistically secure, the reader's attention moves to content and organisation, and small language slips become less salient [reasoned extension].

R6. One feature contaminates the others (halo). In experiments with manipulated texts:

  • Spelling errors lowered teachers' judgments of "almost all textual characteristics" of L2 essays [source: Jansen et al. 2021, Frontline Learning Research 9(1)].

  • Orthography and vocabulary were "the two main triggers of halo effects" [source: Trüb et al. 2026, Assessing Writing, 101036].

  • Greater lexical diversity and sophistication raised overall ratings and also the "grammar" and "essay frame" ratings [source: Vögelin et al. 2019, Assessing Writing 39, abstract].

  • Better cohesive devices and paragraphing raised all analytic scales, more strongly when overall text quality was low [source: Vögelin et al. 2020, Cogent Education 7(1), abstract].

  • Caveat: these raters were pre-service teachers, not trained TEF correctors. Training reduces halo but rarely removes it [reasoned].

R7. Length is evidence. On TOEFL essays, length correlates with human scores (average Pearson about 0.59 in one analysis), and an ETS report found that high holistic–analytic correlations were largely due to length [source: 2021 SustaiNLP paper; ETS 2008 report on TOEFL CBT analytic scoring; both via search summaries]. IELTS descriptors say "Length may be insufficient to provide evidence of control of sentence forms" [source: IELTS Writing band descriptors, updated May 2023]. Length matters because it is the denominator for every judgment of control [reasoned].

R8. Copied and memorised language is discounted. IELTS: "Any copied rubric must be discounted"; low bands mention "over-dependence on input material or memorised language"; band 0 applies where "a candidate's answer has been totally memorised" [source: IELTS descriptors 2023]. In 233 IELTS scripts, copied prompt material and "generic" nativelike text stayed flat across bands (it did not distinguish levels), while non-generic nativelike text rose with band; the authors accept some pre-memorised material, judged "in the context of the candidate's overall performance" [source: Wray & Pegg 2009, IELTS Research Reports]. Raters at a large test centre "give the test taker the benefit of the doubt" the first time they see a template and recognise it after "seeing the nearly identical language multiple times"; human raters were sometimes more aggressive than the detection model [source: Samant et al. 2025, arXiv:2509.08355].

R9. Slips and systems are judged differently. CEFR B2 grammatical accuracy: "Good grammatical control; occasional 'slips' or non-systematic errors and minor flaws in sentence structure may still occur, but they are rare and can often be corrected in retrospect" and "Does not make mistakes which lead to misunderstanding". B1: "Errors occur, but it is clear what he/she is trying to express", with "noticeable mother tongue influence" [source: CEFR scales as reproduced by CEFTrain, University of Helsinki]. IELTS band 7 likewise allows "occasional, non-systematic errors" [source: IELTS descriptors 2023]. The classic distinction is between systematic errors (competence) and mistakes (performance slips that can be self-corrected) [source: Corder's distinction as summarised in Wikipedia, "Error (linguistics)"].

R10. Raters weigh things differently. Raters fall into types that weight criteria differently [source: Eckes 2008, Language Testing 25, as described in secondary sources]. Experienced ESL raters were more severe and gave more importance to linguistic accuracy than novices [source: Barkaoui 2010, TESOL Quarterly, abstract via search]. Some scales treat negative features as caps: "A script must fully fit the positive features of the descriptor at a particular level. Bolded text indicates negative features that will limit a rating" [source: IELTS descriptors 2023]. Whether the TEF grid uses caps is not public.

4. A model of the verdict, with numbered principles

P1. The verdict forms early and is then defended

From R1–R3, the grader's belief has a prior (layout, length), forms an intuitive level within the first lines or paragraph, and then mostly tests that hypothesis. From R4, the grader still reads everything. The result is a confirmatory reader: evidence that agrees with the forming verdict is absorbed easily, and evidence against it has to be salient to move it [reasoned]. This is the mechanism that makes "the point" possible. After the point, only salient negative evidence counts, and §5 defines salient negative evidence as "disaster".

A borrowed frame from sequential testing (Wald's SPRT) [reasoned]: each sentence adds a log-likelihood ratio for "≥7" against "<7". The grader's running total starts at the layout prior. The point is where the total is so far above the line that the largest negative drift a non-disastrous continuation can produce cannot bring it back. In chess terms, the box must reach a position that is won against any reasonable play, not merely a better one. A disaster is a blunder.

P2. Each criterion combines evidence in its own way

The criteria do not combine evidence the same way. This is the core of the lens [reasoned]:

Operator Criteria How evidence combines What the box can do
GATE Task conditions: ≥80 / ≥200 words, several paragraphs (A), letter to the newspaper (B), ≥3 arguments (B), news-article continuation (A), on topic Pass/fail on the whole text; a failure is salient and may cap the score (R10) Close every gate that can be closed early. A gate left open can fail in the rest.
MAX (range) Complex structures, tense/mood repertoire, lexical range, nuance Seeing a feature once proves the writer can do it ("can do") Show each feature once, correctly
RATE (control) Grammar accuracy, spelling, agreement, lexical accuracy Errors divided by text; systematic errors count most (R9) Supply a clean denominator and "pre-certify" structures (P6). The rest can still break it.
COVERAGE (development) "Précises, détaillées", "développer, préciser", supporting details More specific content scores higher Put the specific content in the box
SHAPE (organisation) Intro → development → conclusion; joined paragraphs Whole-text shape Build the intro and developed body in the box. Any ordinary ending completes it, so only a missing ending is a disaster.

MAX is not pure: a grader asking "les phrases sont-elles simples, complexes, maîtrisées ?" also notices how often complex sentences occur. One showpiece sentence in a plain text reads as "attempts complex structures" (CLB 6), not "adequate control" (CLB 7) [reasoned]. That gives P5.

P3. Definition of the point

Let B be a box (a prefix of the text) and C any continuation in the non-disaster set (§5). The point is reached at the shortest B such that, for a strict corrector, every B + C reads at NCLC 7 or above [reasoned definition]. In practice that means all of these hold at the end of B:

  1. every GATE that can close early is closed (P4);
  2. every MAX feature a 7 needs has appeared correctly (§3 table);
  3. the RATE denominator is large enough that control is the grader's default belief (P6);
  4. COVERAGE is at "précises" level at least;
  5. the dilution inequality holds for the length Min will actually write (P5).

The point sits wherever the last of these five is met.

P4. Put the gates inside the box, not promises

If the box only promises something ("I will give three reasons…"), an ordinary weak continuation can break the promise, and a broken consigne is the most salient negative evidence there is (R10, §1.5). If the box already contains the gate's content, the rest can only undo it by deleting or contradicting it, which is a disaster by definition [reasoned]. Concretely:

  • A: the box contains at least one paragraph break (so "plusieurs paragraphes" is met inside it), stays in the third-person news voice, and reveals the discovery.

  • B: the box opens as a letter to the newspaper, refers to the statement, states a position, and states all three arguments, each with a reason. Their fuller development can come later.

P5. The box must outweigh an ordinary rest (dilution)

Suppose the rest is not a disaster but ordinary: correct, plain, B1+ to low-B2. The grader's global level is roughly a weighted blend of box and rest, with early text weighted up by anchoring (R2–R3) [reasoned; linear blending is a simplification]. With box quality q_b, rest quality q_r, cut τ, primacy weight k (early words count k times), the box's share f of the final word count must satisfy

f / (1 − f) ≥ (τ − q_r) / ( k · (q_b − τ) )

Illustrative values on the /699 scale (B2 = 400–499). These are assumptions, not measurements [reasoned]:

What the analyst can take from this:

  • Rule of thumb: the box should be at least about 45% of the words Min will actually submit, written at solid B2 with some upper-B2 features. If Min's ordinary writing is reliably clean low-B2 (q_r ≈ 400 or above), about 30–40% is enough.

  • The other lever is the rest's floor, not the box. A rest that is plain but correct (q_r ≈ 400) needs far less box than a rest with B1 errors (q_r ≈ 380). This is why §5 treats an error-dense rest as a disaster: the box can carry a plain rest but not a broken one.

  • Writing much more than the minimum after the point only helps if those extra words are at least as good as the rest's floor. Extra plain words dilute. Extra slip-ridden words dilute faster.

P6. The box sets control as the default belief, and it inoculates

A grader cannot see what Min knows, only the tokens on the page. An error in a structure that appears correctly elsewhere reads as a slip. The same error repeated, or an error in a structure that never appears correctly, reads as a system (R9) [reasoned]. So:

  • Zero errors in the box. Early errors are weighted up (P1), spelling and vocabulary errors spread by halo (R6), and an error in the box makes any later repeat look systematic. Min can reread, so a clean box is achievable. CEFR B2 even builds in correction "in retrospect".

  • Inoculation: the box should contain correct instances of the structures Min will use again in the rest (past participle agreement, the gender of key nouns, the tense pairs). A later slip in a pre-certified structure then reads as a slip, not a system. The rest should reuse the box's structures rather than try new risky ones.

  • No statistical proof is possible. With zero errors in n sentences, the "rule of three" puts the upper bound of the error rate near 3/n, which is useless for n = 6. The box cannot prove control. It can only make control the grader's default, so that only a dense or repeated pattern in the rest overturns it [reasoned].

  • Denominator size: about 5 or more clean sentences in A and 8 or more in B, including at least two correct complex sentences of different types, before a reader's impression of "controlled" is stable [reasoned estimate].

P7. Evidence must be tied to this prompt

The grader is also, in effect, a memorisation detector (R8). Evidence that could have been written before seeing the prompt carries almost no weight: stock openings ("De nos jours…", "Il est indéniable que…"), fancy connectors stacked in line one, set phrases from prep material. Evidence that could only be written about this prompt carries a lot: precise words for this situation, complex sentences whose content is the prompt's people and objects, agreements with this prompt's specific nouns [reasoned, signal-detection view]. Min's box will in fact be prepared in advance, so this principle is a real risk, not a theory:

  • Every complex sentence in the box should carry prompt-specific content.

  • Prompt words count as zero. Use them as anchors only, and add new words around them. Use reference chains with synonyms (the dog → the animal; Mehdi → the retiree; addiction → dependence). EA-019's French prompt already uses septuagénaire, so repeating it is copying.

  • Quoting the B statement once in the opening sentence is natural. Building the letter out of its words is not.

  • Genre formulas (Madame, Monsieur,; Je vous prie d'agréer…) are expected conventions: leaving them out costs a little, and using them earns nothing. They are never the evidence.

P8. Spend the first sentence on the strongest evidence

The first sentence Min writes gets the most attention and the most weight (R1–R3). In A it is the first sentence after the printed opening, and in B the first sentence after the salutation. It should carry the single most diagnostic combination available: a correct complex sentence, a precise prompt-specific word, and the content the reader is waiting for. In A the printed opening ends on "…une découverte bien étrange…", so the reader is waiting to learn what the discovery is. In B the reader is waiting for Min's position on the statement [reasoned].

P9. Keep the box and the rest consistent

If the box reads like C1 and the rest like B1, the reader's best explanation is "memorised opening" (R8). The box is then discounted after the fact, which is the one way a rest without obvious errors can reverse the verdict [reasoned]. So the box should aim at solid B2 with two or three upper-B2 features, at the top of what Min can also produce without the script, and not at C1 showpieces. This also lowers error risk.

P10. Use the cheap halo levers early

Visible paragraphing, well-chosen connectors, clean spelling and two or three precise words raise every criterion, most of all in texts near the boundary (R6). They are cheap in words and visible from the first scan (R1). Put them in the box [reasoned from R1, R6].

P11. Control the ending too (peak–end)

Retrospective judgments weight the strongest moment and the last one heavily (the peak–end rule from judgment research, applied here by analogy; no writing-rater study of it was found) [reasoned]. Raters also re-check a mark against their overall impression at the end (Crisp's "epilogue", R3). The box is the peak. The rest should end on a short, correct, conventional close: an outcome line in A, a one-sentence conclusion and a letter formula in B. It should not end on a trailing or unfinished sentence.

P12. Design for the strict corrector

Two independent correctors, a severity model that only partly corrects (1.3), and raters who prize accuracy (R10) mean the box should be judged by the strictest likely reader, the "correctness" type. That reader forgives plainness but not errors. This adds weight to P6 (zero errors) and argues against padding the box with risky structures [reasoned].

5. "Disaster", defined in observable terms

A disaster is a salient event after the box that a grader could not read as noise. It falls into one of the classes below. The thresholds are [reasoned] unless marked. The analysts should keep them as stated.

D1. Gate failure (task not done).

  • The final text is under the minimum: Section A under 80 of Min's own words, Section B under 200. Copied prompt words are not counted (R8). The TEF penalty is not public; the TCF can return "A1 non atteint" (§1.5).

  • A: the text switches out of the news voice for good (a diary, a letter, first person as Mehdi), or contradicts the printed opening (it was not the dog, not at the lake).

  • B: the rest reverses the stated position without framing the reversal as a concession, or turns into a different topic for a paragraph or more.

  • A text that stops mid-sentence, or with no ending at all (A) or no conclusion at all (B).

D2. Control collapse (the rest breaks the RATE criteria).

  • Any sentence a francophone reader cannot decode on the first read (B2: "does not make mistakes which lead to misunderstanding", R9).

  • The same error type three or more times in the rest, e.g. gender agreement, past participle agreement with être, a common verb's conjugation, or a missing accent on the same frequent word. This reads as systematic.

  • Errors in most sentences of a whole paragraph (roughly one or more per sentence). That is the B1 picture: "errors occur".

  • Narrative tense collapse in A: switching between present and past tenses with no logic.

D3. Credibility break.

  • A sharp drop in level right after the box, as if written by a different person (P9). This is a judgment call, so the safeguard sits in the box (P9), not in the rest.

  • Generic template paragraphs in the rest that do not fit the prompt (R8).

D4. Form and register break.

  • Addressing the newspaper as tu, slang, SMS spelling, emoticons, all-caps lines.
  • Sentences in English, or English words left untranslated.

Not disasters (the box absorbs these): simple sentences; repeated vocabulary; basic connectors (et, mais, puis, alors); a short plain conclusion; one isolated slip every three or more sentences, of different types and not blocking meaning; slightly unnatural, L1-coloured wording (CLB 7 itself allows it); going over the minimum with ordinary text (but see P5 on dilution).

6. Where the point can sit

It cannot sit very early. Three lower bounds bind [reasoned]:

  • A RATE denominator (P6): about 5 or more clean sentences (A) or 8 or more (B).
  • The gates (P4): the paragraph break in A; three reasoned arguments in B.
  • Dilution (P5): about 45% of the final word count.

A two-sentence opening, however brilliant, sets a good prior. It does not lock the verdict, because an ordinary continuation would make up most of the text.

It must sit before the end. By definition the rest still has to exist and reach the minimum. The point must leave room for a rest that can be plain without harm.

Resulting ranges:

  • Section A: about 55–70 words, i.e. roughly 70–90% of the 80-word minimum. Min then writes a short third paragraph (outcome) to reach about 95–110 words. Here the binding constraints are the gate (paragraphs), coverage (the discovery plus one consequence) and the denominator, more than dilution.

  • Section B: about 115–140 words, i.e. roughly 55–70% of the 200-word minimum. Min then writes one argument's fuller development or example, a proposal, a short conclusion and the letter formula, to reach about 220–250 words. Here the binding constraints are the three-argument gate and dilution.

Both ranges are a decision-theoretic estimate. Lens 3 (economy) will find out how few words can carry this evidence, and the per-prompt analyst should take the larger of the two answers.

7. Does Section A's text help Section B?

  1. Arithmetic: yes, partly. There is one writing score for both sections (1.2), so a strong A raises the combined score and can offset a slightly weaker B. The weights are not public (1.5) [source + reasoned].

  2. Reader carry-over: only if one corrector reads both. If the same corrector reads A and then B, A sets the prior for B, through anchoring (R2, R3) and halo across tasks (R6). If correctors are assigned by section, there is no carry-over beyond the arithmetic. Which applies is not public [reasoned].

  3. Evidence sharing: only for writer-level traits. Spelling, agreement and lexical range are properties of the writer, so A's clean evidence supports B's. Argumentation (CEFR B2 "develops an argument") can only be shown in B, and the narrative tense system mostly in A. A cannot stand in for B's argument evidence [reasoned from §3].

  4. B probably dominates. It is at least 2.5 times longer, has more time, and tests the descriptor that most clearly separates B1 from B2 (a simple essay versus a developed argument) [reasoned].

  5. Planning rule: each box must lock its own section on the assumption that the other section is merely non-disastrous. Treat any help from A as margin against grader severity, never as a reason to shrink B's box [reasoned].

8. Checklist for each per-prompt analyst (from the grader's chair)

  1. Layout scan (R1): A shows three visible paragraphs in the final text, two of them inside the box. B shows a salutation, three to five paragraphs and a closing formula.

  2. First sentence (P8): it answers the reader's open question (A: what was found; B: Min's position), in one correct complex sentence with a precise word that is not in the prompt.

  3. Gates closed in the box (P4): A: news voice, discovery revealed, a paragraph break. B: letter to the newspaper, reference to the statement, position, three arguments each with "because / so".

  4. MAX features (§3): A: an explicit time or cause relation between events, anteriority (pluperfect), a relative clause, a reported witness quote (a genre convention, [reasoned]), precise nouns. B: one concession (nuance), one consequence or cause clause, one mood beyond the indicative (conditional or subjunctive), varied connectors, precise nouns.

  5. Prompt-bound (P7): every complex sentence carries this prompt's content; prompt words are not reused except as anchors; there is no generic prep-school line.

  6. Zero-error design (P6, P12): each risky structure appears once, in a form Min has drilled. The box contains correct instances of the structures the rest will reuse.

  7. Level ceiling (P9): solid B2 plus two or three upper-B2 features, and nothing Min could not also produce without the script.

  8. Dilution check (P5): box words ÷ expected total words ≥ about 0.45.

  9. Rest instructions: reuse the box's structures, stay plain and correct, end cleanly (P11), and keep to a modest margin over the minimum.

P1. The 6/7 line is a control line for grammar and spelling, and a range line for vocabulary. NCLC 6 and 7 are both B2 (F6). The cut at 310 splits B2 roughly in half. The descriptors that change between CLB 6 and 7, and between plain B2 and upper B2 (F8, F9), say the grammar changes from attempting complex structures to controlling them. The vocabulary changes from everyday range to wider range plus idiom. [reasoned from F6, F8, F9]

The usable rule: one correct complex structure beats two attempted ones with one wrong. For vocabulary, the opposite holds: show several precise, less common words, because range itself is what is being judged.

3. The compression model

P2. Prove every criterion inside the box (minimax under an unknown grid). The grid's aggregation rule is not public (F4). It might average the criteria, take a median, or weight them. If the box proves "7 or above" on every criterion, any rule that rises with each criterion gives at least 7. If the box leaves one criterion at 6, some rules give 6. So the robust box is a cover: every criterion gets at least one proof. [reasoned] This turns the box into a set-cover problem: choose the fewest words whose features cover all criteria.

P3. Only diagnostic features count. Evidence is a likelihood ratio. For each feature, ask how much more often it appears in a 7 than in a 6.

  • Je pense que, parce que, et/mais, simple subject–verb–object sentences, and words copied from the prompt appear in both. Their ratio is about 1: they cost words and prove nothing.

  • A correct plus-que-parfait, visible subjunctive, dont, gérondif, concession or idiom is rare at 6 and normal at 7 (F9, F10). Their ratio is high.

  • Basic errors, repetition and a "linear sequence" of facts are typical of 6 and below. Their ratio is under 1: they subtract.

Compression means maximising evidence per word while carrying no subtracting features. In Bayesian terms, the grader's log-odds for "7+" is the prior plus the sum of log likelihood ratios of what they see. The box must push that sum past the threshold with margin. [reasoned]

P4. A feature counts only if it is visible in the written form.

  • Il faut que chacun limite looks exactly like the indicative, so the grader cannot credit a subjunctive. Il faut que chacun prenne (vs prend) is visible.

  • Past participle agreement shows only with feminine or plural nouns.

Use this as a switch:

  • For a feature you control, choose the visible form: être, avoir, faire, pouvoir, prendre, savoir in the subjunctive, or nous/vous forms (prenions), and feminine or plural nouns.

  • For a feature you are unsure of, choose the invisible form: a masculine singular noun, so agreement cannot go wrong.

Agreement that "only shows in writing" (jaunie, devenus) is evidence that exists in this test and not in the oral. [reasoned]

P5. Basic errors diagnose a level. Errors in complex structures diagnose an attempt.

  • CEFR A2 is "systematically makes basic mistakes – … mix up tenses and forget to mark agreement".
  • B2 is "complex structures … with some inaccuracy" (F9).

So a wrong noun gender or subject–verb agreement costs more than a wrong dont. Two consequences follow. The box must have no basic errors. And every word that carries no evidence is not neutral but pure risk: each noun is a gender choice, each adjective an agreement, each verb a conjugation. That is the economic case for cutting beyond "Min asked for the least". [reasoned from F9]

P6. Control has to be seen twice. Redundancy protects against noise. A single correct instance might be a memorised chunk; control is inferred from repetition across different structures. Upper B2 coherence asks for a "variety" of linking words.

There is also a noise argument. Your French under time pressure is a noisy channel, and one carrier per criterion fails if that one sentence has an error. Error-correcting codes add redundancy for exactly this reason.

Rule: every criterion has at least two carriers, and at least one of them is low-risk. Connectors need three different types. [reasoned]

P7. A template carries no information. A grader who has read hundreds of scripts can predict Force est de constater que, De nos jours, Il est indéniable que. A phrase that can be predicted tells the grader little about you, so its likelihood ratio falls toward 1 (Lens 2 covers how raters treat memorised phrases). Evidence is what a template could not have produced.

Rule: every complex structure in the box carries content specific to the prompt. No free-standing set phrase longer than about four words is allowed, except the expected letter conventions (salutation, closing). [reasoned]

4. Where evidence is cheap

P8. Let each genre carry the structures it makes natural.

  • A news story naturally needs the passé composé / imparfait contrast, the plus-que-parfait for anteriority, quoted witnesses, the passive, attribution (selon…), and the journalistic conditional for unconfirmed facts (le trésor aurait appartenu à…).

  • An opinion letter naturally needs concession, the subjunctive after il faut / il est temps / il est essentiel que, the conditional for suggestions and hypotheses, nominal phrasing and impersonal frames.

Forcing a subjunctive into the news story costs words and looks like a template. Genre-native structures are cheaper and also score twice, because "following established conventions of the genre" is B2 evidence (F9) and "respect de la situation" is a criterion (F4). [reasoned]

P10. Choose the content for its grammar. What happens in the story, and which arguments you use, is free: the criteria judge clarity, coherence and relevance, not truth. So pick events and arguments whose natural wording requires the target structures, using words whose gender and spelling you know.

  • A find with a past gives the plus-que-parfait and aurait appartenu.
  • A moved witness gives a quote.
  • A common opinion to set aside gives a concession.
  • A remedy gives a subjunctive or a si-clause.

[reasoned]

P11. Prove required counts by announcing them. The box cannot afford three developed arguments. One sentence naming them in parallel noun phrases ("… pour trois raisons : la santé, la vie de famille et le travail") does four things:

  • shows the count of three inside the box;
  • commits the plan;
  • displays organisation and nominal style;
  • turns the rest into filling slots.

Likewise, the Section A box itself contains a paragraph break, so "plusieurs paragraphes" is already shown. [reasoned]

P12. Never spend words restating the prompt. In A the start is printed above your text. Repeating it is zero evidence and reads as padding. In B, refer to the statement in one clause and reformulate it: addiction aux réseaux sociaux becomes dépendance à leurs écrans. Reformulating is lexical evidence; copying is not. [reasoned from F9, vocabulary range]

5. Packing against error risk

P13. Cap the packing: at most two medium- or high-risk structures per sentence, any number of low-risk lexical features, and one short sentence per paragraph. The chance that a sentence is error-free is the product of each structure's chance of being right. Nesting raises each structure's error rate (working memory; the CV notes accuracy "tends to drop during complex tasks"). Overloaded sentences also hurt clarté, a criterion in its own right, and look assembled.

A worked illustration with assumed, not measured, rates: medium-risk structure 15% error, low-risk 5%.

  • 1 medium + 3 low: 73% clean.
  • 2 medium + 3 low: 62% clean.
  • 3 medium + 3 low: 53% clean.

The third medium-risk structure adds less evidence (diminishing returns) than it costs in risk. Mixing a short sentence with complex ones is itself evidence ("phrases simples, complexes"). Useful sentence length is 12–25 French words, with one of 10 or fewer. [reasoned; numbers illustrative]

P14. Use only structures whose rule you know. Rereading fixes slips, not ignorance.

  • A slip (missing accent, a/à, a forgotten -e) is caught on rereading if you know the rule.
  • A knowledge error (agreeing se sont rendu compte, après que + subjunctive) survives any number of rereads.

If you recall the box's French as prepared, knowledge risk is paid in advance and only slips remain. If you adapt it to a different prompt in the family, knowledge risk returns, so the caps still apply.

A shorter box can be reread two or three times in the time a long one is reread once. On a typed test with clicked accents (F7), that pass matters. Compression lowers the error rate of the box itself. [reasoned]

P15. Inoculate: show correctly, in the box, the basic forms the rest will lean on. Upper B2 tolerates "non-systematic errors" (F9). An error is non-systematic when the same form appears correctly elsewhere in the text. So the box should contain correct instances of what your simpler rest will reuse:

  • plural -ent verbs;
  • a passé composé with être and visible agreement;
  • a feminine adjective;
  • a vs à.

A later slip on one of these then reads as a slip, not a gap. This is cheap, since the forms are basic. [reasoned from F9]

  • Passé simple. News reports use the passé composé; the passé simple adds risk without a criterion that needs it. [reasoned]

  • Rare or error-prone structures: subjonctif passé, ne explétif, après que, quoique, auquel/duquel.

7. The word-count floor

P16. Floor = required carriers ÷ safe density. Count the evidence slots each section needs with redundancy (the checklists in section 9).

  • Section A: about 24 slots. Tense set (4), anteriority, mood/genre (2), quote, complex devices (2+), anaphors (2), connectors (3), precise words (5), idiom, reaction, explanation, paragraph break, visible agreement.

  • Section B: about 28 slots, plus content that cannot be compressed: a concrete example needs real words.

Well-built French sentences carry about 0.4–0.5 slots per word. En creusant au pied d'un arbre, l'animal avait déterré un petit coffre rouillé has 7 slots in 14 words. Above about 0.5 the risk cap (P13) is broken. [reasoned]

A cross-check by structure.

  • A needs two paragraphs. A paragraph of one sentence is weak "paragraph structure" (F8), so that means at least four sentences, at 13–16 words each: 52–64 French words.

  • B needs an introduction (reference + position/plan) and one developed argument (claim and detail, consequence, remedy): at least four sentences plus the salutation, 75–95 French words.

The two methods agree. [reasoned]

What this means.

  • The A box is about 70% of A's 80-word minimum: the point sits near the end of a minimal A text.
  • The B box is about 40–45% of B's 200-word minimum: the point sits after the first argument.

The minimums seem set at roughly the length needed to show B2 evidence, at least for A. [reasoned]

Counting. English undercounts the French by about 10–20% (F12), but this box's journalistic French can come out near 1:1. Analysts should draft the French and count it conservatively: count l'animal as one word in case the counter does, and aim for the minimum plus 15% in the full text.

Time check. At a careful 10 words a minute typed with accents:

  • A: box 6 minutes, rest of 30–50 words 5 minutes, two rereads 4 minutes. That fits in 25.
  • B: box 9 minutes, rest of about 120 words 13 minutes, rereads 5 minutes. That fits in 35.

[reasoned; typing speed assumed]

8. Where the box ends, and what counts as disaster

P17. Put the skill up front and leave the rest only compliance. The point is where everything still owed needs no skill. Everything that needs skill must be in the box: controlled complex structures, nuance, genre markers, idiom, the paragraph link, and the first developed argument. What is left must be doable in plain, clean French:

  • reaching the minimum;
  • finishing (A: an ending; B: arguments 2–3 and a conclusion);
  • the closing formula.

This works like a chess opening that wins material: the conversion needs no brilliant moves, only no blunders. In legal terms, the box makes a prima facie case on every criterion, and the rest only has to avoid contradicting it. [reasoned]

What the rest should look like.

  • Plain simple and compound sentences with ordinary connectors (puis, quand, parce que, donc, ensuite, enfin).
  • Same register, same person, same tenses.
  • No new hard structures. Extra complexity adds little once range is shown, and it adds risk.
  • Not a stream of four-word sentences either: a sharp drop in style tells the grader the box was recited.
  • Length: minimum plus 10–20%.
  • A rest: one closing paragraph of 2–3 sentences (the outcome, a last detail or comment).
  • B rest: arguments 2 and 3, each in its own paragraph of at least two sentences (a claim with a reason, then an example or consequence); a conclusion (Pour toutes ces raisons…) restating the position; Veuillez agréer, Madame, Monsieur, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées.

Disaster: what the rest can do that would pull the score below 7. In observable terms:

  1. Too short. The French text (the A box plus the rest; the whole B letter) is under 80 / 200 words. This breaks the instruction.

  2. A required part is missing. In A, no ending, or the story is abandoned. In B, an announced argument is absent or a bare claim with no reason, there is no conclusion, or the text contradicts the stated position.

  3. The same basic error is repeated (two or three times or more): noun gender, subject–verb agreement, être/avoir in the passé composé, tense drifting between present and past in A, a/à, et/est, ces/ses, -é/-er. Repeated basic errors are A2/B1 evidence (P5), and next to a clean box they make the box look memorised.

  4. A sentence whose meaning a reader cannot work out.

  5. Register break: tu to the newspaper, slang, SMS spelling, English words.

  6. Genre break in A: narrating as je outside quotes, or contradicting the given start (a cat instead of the dog, no lake).

  7. Padding: copying the prompt, repeating the box, or a long, error-heavy text. Some raters penalise weak texts more as they get longer (F11).

Not a disaster:

  • a predictable ending, a formulaic conclusion, plain vocabulary;
  • arguments 2–3 thinner than argument 1, as long as each has a reason;
  • one to three isolated slips in different areas;
  • a length just over the minimum.

[reasoned from F2, F9, F11]

9. Two sections, one score

One global level covers both texts (F4), and the way the two combine is not published. [unverified]

  • Evidence about argument (position, concession, development, letter form) can only come from B.
  • Evidence about narration (tense system, genre conventions of a news story) can only come from A.
  • General language evidence (lexis, syntax, spelling) might carry over if the graders judge across both texts, but nothing guarantees it.

Any A box may meet any B box on exam day, so each box must prove 7 on its own section. Then every pairing proves

  1. The cost is small: in B, the moods and connectors that general evidence needs come with argument anyway (P8). Within a section, the A box does not help the B box. [reasoned, P2]

10. Construction method (for each prompt's analyst)

  1. Inventory the prompt. List the given words: they cannot serve as evidence (P12). List the given facts: they must stay consistent. Note the hook: what the "…" promises in A, and whom the statement targets in B.

  2. Choose content for its grammar (P10).

    • A: the find → a concrete detail → a reaction in quotes → an action or authority with an uncertain explanation. The outcome is left to the rest.

    • B: a position (pick the side whose three arguments are easiest in simple French), a common view to concede, three arguments nameable as nouns, argument 1 with an everyday concrete detail and a remedy.

  3. Lay out the slots.

    • A: ¶1 S1 [pick-up + anteriority + detail + gérondif or venir de]; S2 [background imparfait + dont/où + detail]. ¶2 S3 [quote + inverted speech tag + idiom]; S4 [passé composé action + selon + journalistic conditional].

    • B: Madame, Monsieur, / S1 [reference, reformulated] / S2 [concession + conditional + position + three-item plan] / ¶ S3 [Tout d'abord + claim + concrete detail + ce qui consequence] / S4 [remedy: visible subjunctive or si-clause].

  4. Write the English using the seeding table (section 6). Keep it specific to the prompt, with no free-standing templates (P7) and no gendered je adjectives.

  5. Visibility check (P4). Picture the French. Every claimed structure must show. If it doesn't, change the verb or noun.

  6. Risk check (P13, P14). At most two medium- or high-risk structures per sentence. Nothing from the leave-out list. Every noun of known gender.

  7. Deletion test. Delete each word or phrase in turn. If no checklist item is lost and the sentence stays clear and grammatical, the deletion stands. Then try merging neighbouring sentences: keep any merge that saves three or more words without breaking step 6.

  8. Redundancy check (P6). List the carriers for each criterion. Wherever there is only one, add the cheapest zero-risk carrier (usually a precise word or a reference-back noun).

  9. Count. English words, plus the French count drafted conservatively. Note how many words the rest still owes.

  10. Write the rest spec and the disaster list (section 8) for that prompt.
  11. Write the explanation from steps 7–8: what each element proves, and which criterion would lose its only (or second) carrier if it were removed.

Checklist for the Section A box:

  • [ ] It continues straight on from the printed sentence, with no restating.
  • [ ] At least two nouns refer back instead of repeating (l'animal, le retraité…).

  • [ ] At least one passé composé event, at least two imparfait, and anteriority once (plus-que-parfait / venir de / après avoir + participle).

  • [ ] A journalistic conditional with a source (selon…).

  • [ ] A quote in « » with an inverted speech tag.

  • [ ] At least two complex devices from: dont/où, gérondif, participial clause, infinitif passé, alors que / dès que.

  • [ ] At least three linking devices of different types.

  • [ ] At least five precise words not taken from the prompt, plus one idiom or fixed collocation.
  • [ ] One reaction and one explanation or consequence.
  • [ ] Two paragraphs, the second linked to the first.
  • [ ] At least one visible feminine or plural agreement.
  • [ ] Third person, neutral press register.

Checklist for the Section B box:

  • [ ] Madame, Monsieur,
  • [ ] A one-clause reference to the statement, reformulated.
  • [ ] A clear position plus a concession.
  • [ ] Three arguments announced.
  • [ ] Argument 1 developed: claim, concrete detail, consequence, remedy.
  • [ ] At least one visible subjunctive.
  • [ ] At least one conditional.
  • [ ] At least two complex devices.
  • [ ] At least four connector types.
  • [ ] At least five precise topic words not in the prompt, plus one idiom/collocation and one noun-based phrase.
  • [ ] Each prompt keyword used at most once.
  • [ ] vous plus one impersonal formal frame.
  • [ ] Two paragraphs.

Elements:

What you asked, and what is here

You put seven transcripts of YouTube mock TEF Canada speaking tests in Downloads and asked, in short:

  1. What are they doing that you aren't, and what are you doing that isn't needed because they don't do it? Too much or too little, and how much emphasis does each area need?
  2. Each demo side by side, English (natural) on the left, French on the right, to see what they say and how they introduce themselves, start off and respond.
  3. Why each one is the level its title claims, checked against the logic of the NCLC 7 floor page, and scored that way if it fits.

What is here. The seven files are six distinct demos: the "C1C2 Part A BEST Samples" video repeats the Section A of the C2 and C1 videos (note at the bottom). Five levels come from the video titles; the two French School TV levels (C1 and C1) are from the thumbnails, as you told me. Each demo has its verdict, then notes per section (opening, structure, responses, close), the four grid criteria rated against the floor, where "the point" falls, the disasters checked, and the full transcript. In the transcript, a coloured chip on a candidate turn is its rung on the floor's reply ladder (P7), and ◆ The point marks the turn after which the floor says only a pattern of trouble could pull that section down.

How it was scored. One Opus subagent per demo read the floor page's first principles and standards (nclc7-floor/build/FIRST_PRINCIPLES.md, STANDARDS.md) and held the transcript to them: the reply ladder, the marked structures and links, precise words, the five conditions of the point, D1-D8. I then checked every verdict against the transcripts.

Limits. The levels in the titles are the channels' claims, not CCI results. Only transcripts were read, so delivery (pace, pronunciation) is judged from hesitations and broken-up sentences only. The ads are not visible, so where a candidate seems to read the ad's own words, that is a judgment. The videos may be edited, so a short section may have been cut.

Does the NCLC 7 floor logic fit these demos?

Yes, with an offset, so every demo is scored that way. The floor logic ranks the six demos in the same order as the channels' titles, and it sits half a level to a level below every title. Taking each demo's best section: the C2 demo's Section B is the only one at C1; the three C1-titled performances (French School TV's A and B, Mocko's C1) reach B2 upper; the two B2-titled ones reach B2 lower. Same order, one step down.

Why the offset. The floor answers a different question from a channel title. It asks for the least you can say after which no examiner could place you below NCLC 7, so it wants every criterion "beyond doubt" (F3, P15b) and every condition of the point met. A title is a teacher's overall impression, and the levels themselves are the channels' claims, not CCI results. So read the floor's label as the box the evidence locks in, and a rater's usual judgment as somewhat above it. For you that is the right direction: a performance the floor calls B2 upper is one the channels call C1.

Two ways to combine the sections. The table's "Floor overall" takes the weaker section, because the floor's P16 says each section must clear the bar alone. TEF's real formula for combining them is not public, so the table also gives the plain average. It matters most for the C2 demo: B2 lower on the weaker section, B2 upper on the average.

Where the demos confirm the floor

  1. The level sits in the replies (P4, P7). In every demo the turns that build on the examiner's last words lift the level, and list questions earn almost nothing. The C2 candidate's Section A asks 14 clean questions with no follow-up and scores B1 on the task. French School TV's call chases every vague answer and scores C1 on the task.
  2. Ending the call yourself costs (D5). The two weakest Section As are the two where the candidate closed early: B2 #1 at 2:42 and the C2 call at 3:16.
  3. Objection replies follow the floor's shape (P17b). Every Section B at B2 upper or above concedes a part, counters with a concrete fact and keeps going when pushed again. The second B2 demo's Section B breaks down there.
  4. Accuracy separates the middle from the top. Mocko's C1-titled candidate and the second B2 candidate make errors in common structures in most turns ("je sais pas qu'est-ce que", "il s'agit sur", "quel son handicap"). That holds their syntax at B2 lower while their interaction is fine, exactly as the grid's B2 / C1 descriptors split it.

Where the demos push back on the floor

  1. Links in Section A (P8b and the link condition of the point). None of the five Section As shows three kinds of link, including the two the channels rate highest. A call made of questions rarely needs "even if" or "on the other hand". Three of the five scorers who saw a Section A flagged this on their own, and it is the main reason "the point" is never reached in most Section As here. Read the link rule as a Section B rule; in Section A, count the follow-ups instead.
  2. Hard structures (P8). The two strongest Section Bs reach C1 syntax with almost no subjunctive, dont or si + imparfait. Clefts, soit… soit, the gérondif and varied links did the work.
  3. Turn length in B (P14's 15 to 40 words). The best replies run 45 to 155 words without turning into monologues.
  4. Presenting first in B (P17b). French School TV teaches drip-feeding the ad, one fact per objection, and in three of the four Mocko Section Bs the friend objects after the first sentence about the ad anyway. The floor's own P18 ("don't spend the reserve") points the same way. Two or three sentences of presentation are enough.
  5. Invented facts and clichés (P18, P10). French School TV's Marie invents a company with fifteen years of history, insect nets, good reviews and a French-speaking guide, and repeats incroyable, inoubliable, magique. She still gets a C1 title and the examiner never objects. These two rules protect you from risk; they are not where points are lost. A real examiner knows the ad and can call out an invented fact, so keep hedging.

Two cautions about the demos themselves

  • The C1 and second B2 Mocko candidates are probably the same person. They share 3.3% of their three-word phrases; every other pair shares 1.3% or less. They open both sections with nearly the same words ("j'ai trouvé cette… annonce", "Salut, ça va ? … Comment s'est passé…") and both bring up their children. The transcripts can't prove it, but the audio files in Downloads would. If so, one speaker carries a C1 title in one video and a B2 title in the other, so treat the titles as rough. The floor gives that speaker B2 lower and B1 upper, which fits one person on two days.
  • The demo examiners are gentler than the publisher's booklet (F5). In two of the five Section As (the C2 and second B2 calls) the examiner answers almost everything in full, so there is no "it depends" to chase, and one friend gives in ("T'as gagné"). Expect the real examiner to push harder.

Add (they do it, you don't yet)

  1. The follow-up on a vague answer (Section A). French School TV's narrator says it outright: the examiner gives vague information on purpose to see whether you ask for precision (also the floor's F5). French School TV's call does it three times: price ("c'est en fonction de ça que varient les tarifs ?", then "donc 30 euros, ce serait pour les deux activités et 20 euros pour une activité ?"), hours ("est-ce que vous pouvez me préciser… est-ce que ça ferme le midi ?"), parking ("c'est à la journée, c'est à l'heure ?"). Have five frames you can say without thinking: Ça dépend de quoi, exactement ? · Donc, si je comprends bien, X ; dans ce cas, Y ? · Vous pouvez me préciser… ? · Et si [their condition], qu'est-ce qui se passe ? · Ah, donc je dois prévoir… Dans ce cas, … ? (the B2 caller starts this with "je dois prévoir de la monnaie" but changes topic; the follow-up question is what makes it a B2 move).
  2. Enough short questions to fill five minutes. The three calls that ran until the examiner (or the five minutes) ended them asked about 18 questions each, one per turn; the two that ran out (9 and 14 questions) were closed early by the candidate. More questions don't raise the level on their own (the C2 call's 14 clean questions scored B1 on the task); they keep the call alive while the follow-ups do the work. Have 15 or more ready, each anchored in the ad or your situation.
  3. Replies that start on the friend's words and stop (Section B). "Mais justement, on va s'habituer", "Ben en fait, si, il y a beaucoup de monde, mais ça montre bien que c'est utile", "Ne t'inquiète pas pour ton corps". Then one fact from the ad (12 people maximum, the first day has no activity, a 3-star hotel), then hand back. The friend pushes again, which is what you want (P6: the second reply is what "defends firmly" sounds like).
  4. Keep the ad facts in reserve. French School TV's rule: present the ad's information bit by bit, because if you give it all at once you have nothing left to argue with. The floor says the same (P18). Present in two or three sentences; spend each other fact as the answer to an objection.
  5. Keep going until the examiner ends it. "C'est toujours l'évaluateur qui termine" (French School TV). Two candidates closed their own call early: B2 #1 at 2:42 and the C2 candidate at 3:16 ("vous avez répondu à toutes mes questions"). Those are the two weakest Section As on the floor (D5).

Drop or shrink (you do it, they don't)

  1. The long formal opener. Cut the A opener to one sentence of about 15 words that names the ad and asks the first question (the floor's STANDARDS #11 says the same). The phrases you'd cut are the prep-book kind the floor says carry no evidence (P3).
  2. Context before questions. Your "My situation", "What I want", "What I assume or don't know" and "Worries" cards (78) frame the call. The demos give their situation only when it creates a condition the examiner has to answer: a group of 15, a wheelchair user, "j'ai des enfants" (so, can I make up a missed class?), "je suis nulle en danse" (so, is there a level test?). Keep one or two such hooks per call and drop the rest.
  3. Reciting arguments. In B, nobody in the demos delivers a run of arguments before the friend objects. Keep the argument bank as a store, but drill it as answers to objections: hear the objection, say one argument from the bank, stop. The arguments used were the ones the ad's subject and the friend's objection called for (the e-cigarette: health and money; the bulbs: money and the planet; the café: learning and cost), and money came up in all five.
  4. Trust, experience and safety questions in A (77 cards). The demos rarely ask them. Keep a few; they are not where the level is decided.

The frame: what pushes a response further

Forget the exam. Each note below takes one speaking prompt as the real situation (a five-minute call to the business, or a talk with the friend in the intro) and works out what it takes to finish it, and how far it can be pushed. One prompt per family group, the most frequent of each group; one subagent per note.

A real exchange exists to move a situation to its next step. It is complete when that next step can happen without another exchange. Past correct grammar and vocabulary, four levers decide how far a speaker gets:

  1. Complete: everything the next step depends on is covered. When the call or conversation ends, no missing fact forces a second call, and no unanswered doubt leaves the other person at "maybe".
  2. Developed: each point is carried as far as it goes: the claim, the reason, the mechanism, the concrete consequence, a scene or example, and "and if not, then what". A question is developed when it carries why you ask it and you already know your follow-up for each likely answer.
  3. Targeted: every line is aimed at this listener, this goal, this moment of the conversation. The order serves the goal. Nothing is said because it is usually said.
  4. Specific (the exact shade): the words could only belong to this situation: the ad's own lines, this person's circumstances, numbers, names, and the precise nuance. Not "is the price all-in?" but "does the price cover firing and glazing, and do I take the bowls home the same day?" (a pottery class).

Portability test: if a sentence could move unchanged to another ad, it belongs to no ad. Replace it or cut it.

How the frame was derived

The four lever names are yours: in your request you named developed, targeted, complete, and specific to the exact shade. What I derived is what each one means, why those four cover the ground once grammar and vocabulary stop being the problem, and a test for each that can be run sentence by sentence.

1. Start from what speaking is for

People speak to change something outside the sentence: to get information they lack, or to get someone to do something. J. L. Austin (How to Do Things with Words, 1962) separated what a sentence says from the effect it has on the listener. Only the effect counts as the result. So "done" is defined by a state of the world after the exchange, and the language is judged by whether it gets there.

That gives the definition used on every note: a call or conversation is complete when its next step can happen without another exchange. In a call to a business, the next step is a decision you can act on (book, order, enrol). With a friend, it is the friend acting (calling the number, picking dates).

2. Take away what is already solved

Once grammar and vocabulary no longer cause misunderstanding, two answers that are both correct can still differ a lot in how well they reach the goal. The difference is in what is said: which points, how far each goes, who they are aimed at, how exactly they fit. Fancy words and structures change how a point is encoded, not whether it moves the listener to the next step, so they drop out of the frame.

3. Split "what is said" into independent parts

Compare any answer with the ideal answer for that situation. The gap can only be in four places, and each can be bad while the others are good:

  • Breadth (complete): points the next step needs are missing. A caller can ask brilliant questions about equipment and never ask the date of the next session.
  • Depth (developed): a point is there but stops at the bare claim. "It's cheap" stops early. The developed version gives the reason, the mechanism, the concrete consequence, a scene, and what happens if it goes the other way.
  • Direction (targeted): a point is true and developed but chosen by habit, aimed at nobody in particular, or said at the wrong moment of the conversation.
  • Resolution (specific): a point is aimed right but blurry. For a boat rental, "Is it safe?" versus "if the wind picks up after I've taken the boat out, who tells me to come back, and do I get the lost hour back?"

These line up with H. P. Grice's conversational maxims ("Logic and Conversation", 1975): quantity (say as much as the exchange needs) covers breadth, relation (be relevant) covers direction, manner (be clear, avoid vagueness) covers resolution. Grice's fourth maxim, quality (say only what you have evidence for), became the brief's rule against inventing facts about the business: in a call, unknowns become questions; with a friend, an argument that needs a fact the ad does not give becomes something you check together. Depth has no Grice maxim; it comes from your own point that past a certain level the difference is how developed the answer is.

Register (how you talk to a staff member versus a friend) is folded into direction, because it is part of aiming at this listener. That is a judgment call; it could be a fifth lever.

4. Give each lever a test

A lever with no test turns into a slogan, so each one got a check that can be run on a single line:

  • Complete: when it ends, is there a missing fact that forces a second call, or a doubt that leaves the friend at "maybe"?
  • Developed: ask "and then?" of each point until it reaches a concrete consequence or a scene in this person's life, and "and if not?" until each likely answer has a follow-up.
  • Targeted: why this line, to this person, at this moment?
  • Specific: the portability test. If a sentence could move unchanged to another ad, it tells the listener nothing about this ad: a line that fits every situation carries no information about which situation it is in. That is the exact form of your "this prompt and this prompt only". The hardest version of the test is the other ads in the same family group, because they share most of the situation; each subagent got those ads to test against.

5. Where the ceiling is

Each lever's limit is set by the situation, not by the language:

  • Completeness stops at what the next step needs.
  • Development is limited by time (five minutes on the phone) and by how much the listener will take before it becomes a lecture.
  • Targeting is limited by what you know about the person: the intro's one line about the friend, or what you tell the staff member about yourself in the first sentence.
  • Specificity is limited by the material: the ad's own lines and the intro's description of the person.

So "the furthest extent" means using up that material: every line of the ad read for what it promises, what it leaves out, and what it implies for this person. The levers also compete. In five minutes, more breadth means less depth, so each Section A note says which answers you need if the call is cut after ninety seconds, and depth goes to the points the next step depends on.

Task completion

The next step is a booking, and the ad says to make it on this phone line. When you hang up, a date for the introduction is held under your name, and you know the total you will pay and what it covers, where to be and at what time, what to wear, and what happens to that date and your money if the wind says no. You also know, in the staff member's own words, what "a series of mini-flights" means on their slope, so you are booking the flight you actually want.

Who is calling

The most natural caller is you: an adult who has never flown anything, reasonably fit, who wants to be the one under the wing, free on weekends over the next month or so, possibly bringing one friend. Have your weight and height in front of you before you dial. A school sizes the wing to your weight and will ask.

A different caller changes the call:

  • Buying it as a gift: the person who will fly is not on the line, so their weight and age come first, because weight decides whether the school has a wing for them. Then ask how long the voucher stays valid and whether dates lost to wind extend it, whether the name on it can change, and how the recipient books their own day.
  • A parent booking for a teenager: minimum age, whether a parent signs on site, and minimum weight, since a light fourteen-year-old can fall below the smallest wing the school owns.
  • Someone who mainly wants the view from up high: this programme may be the wrong product. Ask about a tandem flight with an instructor and treat everything below as the comparison.
  • A visitor with one free weekend: the weather rule outranks the price. A wind-cancelled day moved to next month is worth nothing to you, so ask about a refund before anything else.

Reading the ad as questions

"On the way to the clouds…" The headline sells height. The programme underneath sells "mini-flights." Those two may not meet on your first day. How high do the mini-flights go, and what is the next step for someone who wants the clouds?

"Discover new sensations" The sensation that counts is the moment the wing takes your weight and your feet leave the slope. Does every student reach that moment on the day? If some don't, is it the wind, or how well they control the wing on the ground?

"your introduction to paragliding" An introduction leads somewhere. How long is it (half day, full day, two days), and is it the first block of a course that ends with you flying alone from a real launch?

"Ground training" In this sport, ground training usually happens in a field: you learn to pull the wing up over your head and keep it there while you walk, turn and run. How long does it last, and how much theory comes with it (wind, launch and landing rules, the radio signals you will follow)?

"Practical trials" The vaguest line in the ad. It could mean inflations and short runs where your feet stay down, or first take-offs. Ask them to describe what you physically do in that part.

"Series of mini-flights" Five unknowns in one line: how many, how high, how long each, alone or attached to an instructor, and from what slope. Plus one the ad never hints at: every flight ends at the bottom of the slope, so how do you and the wing get back to the top?

"BOOK AT" Booking is by phone. This call can be the booking, so be ready with your name, contact details and a card if they want a deposit.

"Exceptional rates!" Compared to what: their tandem price, their full course, last season, a weekday? Per person or per group? Until when? What is inside it: wing, seat, helmet, radio, insurance or a day membership, a ride back up the slope? Paid when, and returned when the wind cancels?

What the ad leaves out entirely: where the slope is, which season and which days, the times, the age and weight limits, health limits, what to bring, group size, who the instructors are and what they are certified to teach, insurance, and weather.

The call, in order

  1. Open with who you are and what you want. "Hi, I'm calling about the introduction to paragliding. I've never flown, I'm fairly fit, I weigh about [your weight], and I'd like to do it on a Saturday in the next few weeks, maybe with a friend. Can I ask a few quick questions and then book?" "Never flown" and your weight are the two facts that change every answer after this: which wing, which group, how many flights.

  2. What the flights are. "When you say a series of mini-flights, am I flying on my own, how high do beginners usually get, and about how many flights do people get in the day?"

    • If they say you fly alone a few metres above a training slope, guided by radio: that is the classic first day. Ask, "Who's on the radio? Is there one instructor at the top and one at the bottom?"
    • If they say you fly with an instructor: ask how high and how long, and whether you also handle the wing yourself at some point.
    • If the number of flights "depends": ask on what. If it is wind, go to the weather questions. If it is your ground handling, ask, "What happens if I'm not ready to fly by the end of the day? Do I come back for the flights at no charge?"

  3. The dates. "What are the next dates with space? Is it one day or two, and what time do we start and finish?" Then: "Do you fly beginners through the middle of the day, or is there a break while the air gets bumpy?" Many schools put beginners in the calm morning and evening air, so a "day" can be two sessions with a long gap, and you need to know whether to bring lunch or a book.

  4. The price, all in. "What's the total per person? Does it include the wing, seat, helmet and radio? Is there any insurance or membership on top?"

    • If insurance or membership is extra: ask how much, and whether it covers your injuries or only their liability.
    • Then the headline: "What makes it an exceptional rate? Is it a promotion, and until when? If I book now for a date next month, do I keep that price?"
    • Then the money flow: "How much do you need to hold a date, and when is the rest due?"

  5. The weather rule. "Who decides whether the day goes ahead, and when do you tell me: the evening before or the morning of?"

    • If they cancel, ask: "Do I move to another date, or can I get my money back?" If it is reschedule only, ask how long the credit lasts.
    • If they decide on the hill at the meeting time: ask whether a day lost after you have driven out is moved at no cost.
    • Then the insider question: "If it's too windy to fly but fine for ground handling, do we do the ground part that day and come back for the flights?" That turns a lost day into half a day done.

  6. Your body. "What's the weight range? Is there a minimum or maximum age?" Then: "Does anything rule people out, like back trouble or an old ankle injury? Is there a health form to sign?" Then the question the ad hides: "After each flight, do we walk back up with the wing, or is there a vehicle?" Carrying several kilos of wing and seat up a slope, again and again, decides how tired you will be and how many flights you fit in. If it is a walk, ask how long the walk is.

  7. The day itself. "Where exactly do we meet, and is it the same place we fly?" If they use different slopes depending on the wind direction, the meeting point may only be fixed the evening before; ask how they will send it. Then: "What should I wear and bring?" Expect boots with ankle support (landings are where beginners turn ankles), long sleeves and long trousers, gloves, sunglasses, water and food. If they say running shoes are fine, ask again, because that answer tells you something about the school.

  8. Who teaches. "Who are the instructors, and are they certified to teach beginners? How many students per instructor?" A small group means more flights each. One instructor for a large group means more waiting at the top with the wing packed.

  9. After the introduction. "If I like it, what's the next step toward flying from higher up? Does the introduction count toward that course, and does its price come off?" If it counts, ask how long that credit stays valid.

  10. Recap and close, as below.

Steering the five minutes

The ad sells sensations, and the person who answers may well be an instructor who loves describing them. Give them one sentence, then pull back with the next concrete question: "That sounds great. Practically, how high do we get?" If they start describing the full course, park it: "Good to know for later. For the introduction, what's the next date?"

Keep the order: what the flights are, the date, the total, the wind rule. Everything after those four improves the booking but does not make or break it.

If the person who answers is not an instructor and cannot say how high the flights go or who is on the radio, ask for an instructor to call you back that evening, and book the date anyway with the deposit refundable until that call happens.

At about four minutes, recap in one breath: "So that's Saturday the [date], meet at [place] at [time], done by about [time], [total] each including the wing, helmet and radio, [deposit] to hold it, you text me by [time] the evening before if the wind cancels, and then I either move to [backup date] or get refunded. Boots and long sleeves. Is that right?"

Close with something that holds: your name on the date, the deposit paid or a deadline to pay it, a written confirmation by email or text with the meeting point and the wind rule in it, the name of the person you spoke to, and the time and channel of the weather decision.

If the call is cut at ninety seconds

You need three answers by then: that the mini-flights are what you want (you, a wing, a slope, or else a tandem), the next date with space, and the total per person. Ask for the date to be held and for the weather rule, meeting point and kit list to come in the confirmation message. If your weight or age is near the edge of what a school might accept, ask that before anything else, because a no there ends the call.

Pushing it further

  • A backup date in the same call. Wind cancels paragliding more than almost any other booking. Ask to hold Saturday with Sunday, or the following Saturday, pencilled in as the fallback, so a cancellation becomes a one-text move instead of a new call.
  • The short-notice list. "If a weekday comes up with good conditions and a free place, can you call me?" Schools that live by the forecast fill gaps at the last minute. If you can take a day off on a day's notice, say so; it may get you flying sooner than any weekend.
  • How often your month gets cancelled. "Roughly how many introduction days do you lose to wind in [month]?" The answer tells you whether to pick a different month before you pay anything.
  • Which way the slope faces. "What wind direction does the training slope need?" With that, you can read the forecast yourself the night before and see the cancellation coming.
  • The clouds in the headline. Ask whether they also run tandem flights from a higher launch, and whether one can be added at the end of the introduction day or at a combined price. That gets you the height the headline sells while the programme keeps you a few metres up.
  • The bring-a-friend price. "If two of us book the same date, is there a better rate?" Then make sure you are in the same group on the same slope, so you can drive out together and watch each other's flights.
  • Your own insurance. Many personal accident and travel policies exclude air sports. Ask the school exactly what their cover pays for, then check your own policy before the day.
  • A camera. "Can I wear a camera on the helmet, or do you take photos?" Better to hear the rule on the phone than at the top of the slope.
  • If you freeze. "If I get nervous on the day, can I do the ground training and come back another day for the flights?" The answer shows how the school treats first-timers.

Task completion

You hang up booked into a first class at Danse Académie on a named date, either as a trial or already enrolled. You know the class exactly as it is listed on danseacademie.org, its day and time, whether that first session starts from the very first step, whether you need to bring a partner, what you pay and what that covers, what to wear on your feet, and the street address, which the ad never gives. If any of those is still a guess, you either call back or turn up on the wrong evening in the wrong shoes.

Who is calling

The ad speaks to two people at once: someone who wants to learn and someone who wants to improve. Take the first. You are an adult who has never taken a dance class, or took a few years ago and remembers nothing. You work days, so you can come on weekday evenings from about seven, straight from work, or on Saturday morning. You would come on your own. That last fact shapes the call more than any other, because many dances are taught in couples, and a beginner who arrives alone in a partner class depends entirely on how the school handles partners.

A different caller changes the call in specific places:

  • You are coming as a couple. The partner questions disappear. Both your schedules have to fit the same slot, and you ask whether there is a price for two.
  • You already dance (the ad's "improve your skills"). The call becomes about placement. Describe what you can do in concrete terms ("two years of salsa, I'm fine with basic turns, lost on anything with a spin pattern"), ask how they decide your level, and ask to try the level above and drop down if it moves too fast.
  • You have a date to dance for, such as a wedding. Count the weeks out loud on the call. A group class on a term schedule may not get you there; ask about private lessons and whether a teacher will work to your own song.
  • Your hours change from week to week. "Flexible Schedules" becomes the whole call, because one fixed evening is useless to you.

What each line of the ad leaves open

"Do you want to learn to dance? To improve your skills?" It promises a place for complete beginners. It does not promise that a beginner class is starting when you call. Most dance schools run in terms, and a beginner group that began six weeks ago has already built a sequence of steps you have never seen; for you it is no longer a beginner class. The real question is when the next class that starts from zero begins. The second sentence tells you there are levels above beginner, so there is somewhere to go after the first term.

"Come and enroll at our school Danse Académie!" "Enroll" and "school" point to a commitment, a term or a year, possibly with a registration fee on top of the classes. Left out: how long you sign up for, what joining part-way through costs, and what happens if you twist an ankle in week three.

"Different types of dances" The ad does not name a single one. The only styles that matter to you are those with a beginner class at a time you can come. Inside that short list, the split that matters is partner dances, where you need someone to dance with, and solo styles, where you don't.

"Flexible Schedules" This could mean several time slots to choose from when you enroll, the freedom to switch slots week to week, or a pass you use when you like. For someone whose workday sometimes runs past six, these are three different products. And flexibility has a hidden cost in dance: classes build a routine week on week, so missing two in a row can leave you a full sequence behind.

"Themed evenings" Social nights. Unknown: the themes, how often they happen, whether they are included in enrolment or paid separately, whether beginners are welcome, and whether you can bring someone who isn't enrolled. For a beginner they matter more than they look, because the social floor is where steps learned with the same few classmates get tried with strangers to real music.

"Warm atmosphere!" You cannot hear warmth over the phone, but you can test it: how many people are in the beginner class, what happens to a newcomer who arrives alone, whether partners rotate, and whether you can see a class or an evening before paying.

"Information and registration: www.danseacademie.org" Registration goes through the website. The phone call should leave you knowing exactly which listing on that site to pick, and the cleanest way to get there is to have the site open while you talk.

The call, in order

  1. Opening. "Hi, I'm calling about your ad for Danse Académie. I'd be a complete beginner, I'd be coming on my own, and I'm free weekday evenings from seven and Saturday mornings. Which of your dances have a beginner class at those times, and which one would you put someone like me in?" Three constraints up front stop them from reading out every style and every slot. The last half hands them the recommendation, which staff at a dance school are usually glad to make.

    • If they list several: "Which of those start from zero, and which are already a few weeks in?"
    • If they ask what you'd like: name one partner style and one solo style you'd enjoy (salsa, say, or contemporary), then let them say which fits your evenings.

  2. Where the class is up to. "When is the first session of that class? If it's already running, how many weeks in is it?"

    • Starting within two weeks: write down the date. This is your class.
    • Already running: "Can a beginner still join it? When does the next beginner group start? Would one private lesson catch me up, and what does that cost?"

  3. Partners, if it's a partner dance. "Do I need to bring a partner, or do people change partners during the class?"

    • They rotate: "Is the class balanced right now, or are you short of leaders or followers?" If they are short of the role you'd dance, ask whether there is a waiting list for that role, or whether you could learn the other role.
    • You need your own partner: "Do you keep a list of people looking for one?" If not, move to a solo style. A partner class without a partner is an hour standing at the side of the room.

  4. "Flexible schedules." "Your ad says flexible schedules. Do I choose one fixed class, or can I come to another class at my level if work runs late?"

    • One fixed class: "If I miss a week, can I make it up in another slot, and within how long?"
    • Any class: "Are the different slots at the same point in the routine, with the same teacher?" If not, switching means arriving in the middle of someone else's sequence.
    • A pass: "How many classes, and when does it expire? Does it stop during holidays?"

  5. Trial. "Can I try the actual class I'd be joining, on its normal evening, before I enroll? Is that free, or taken off the price if I sign up?" Trying the real class shows you the room, the pace and the partner balance. A separate taster session shows you none of that.

    • Yes: book it now, with the date.
    • No: "Could I watch the last twenty minutes of a class, or come to the next themed evening first?"

  6. Money and commitment. "What does enrolling cover: a term, the year, a set number of classes? Is there a registration fee on top? If I start part-way through, is the price reduced? If I have to stop because of an injury, what happens to what I've paid?"

    • If the price is per year: ask whether you can pay per term or in instalments.

  7. Themed evenings. "What are the themed evenings, and how often? Are they included when I enroll? Can a beginner come, and can I bring a friend who isn't a student?"

    • If beginners are welcome: ask the date of the next one.

  8. Practical. "What's the exact address, and is there parking or a stop nearby? Is there somewhere to change? I'd be coming straight from work. What should I wear on my feet the first time?"

    • Shoes matter in partner dances: rubber soles grip the floor on turns and wrench knees. If they say dance shoes, ask whether smooth-soled street shoes will do for the first weeks, before you spend money on a pair.

Steering the five minutes

If you can, open danseacademie.org before you dial and glance at whatever timetable it shows. Then the call goes to what a web page cannot tell you: whether the beginner class is really at the beginning, how partners work, and what "flexible" means in practice.

When the staff member starts on the history of the school or the full list of styles, bring it back with one of your constraints: "That sounds good. For a beginner on my own on a weekday evening, which of those?" Each redirect repeats a fact about you, so it sounds like interest in their answer.

At around four minutes, recap with their answers in place. With example answers, it sounds like this: "So, the beginner salsa class on Thursdays at 7:30, first session on the 4th, I'd come alone and you rotate partners, the trial is free, smooth-soled shoes, and you're at this address. Have I got that right?" The recap catches the error you would otherwise discover at the door.

Close:

  • "Can you put me down for the trial now? Who should I ask for when I arrive?"
  • If registration has to go through the site: "I've got your website open. Which listing is it, the one for Thursdays?" Find it while they are still on the line, so any field on the form you don't understand gets answered.
  • "Could you email me the timetable and prices, with my trial date?" Spell your address. Write down the name of the person you spoke to.

If the call is cut at ninety seconds

You need three answers: a class at a time you can come that starts from zero (or the date the next one does), whether you need a partner for it, and whether you can try it first. Prices, themed evenings, shoes and the address can come from the website or a short email. A beginner group that is eight weeks in, or a partner class with nobody to partner you, is something no website will warn you about.

How far it goes

  • Use a themed evening to check the "warm atmosphere." If beginners are welcome and there is a short lesson at the start, as there often is at dance socials, go to the next one before you enroll. In one evening you see the teachers, the regulars and how a newcomer is treated, for the price of an entry or nothing.
  • Ask to be expected. "Could you let the teacher know a beginner is coming on their own? Should I come ten minutes early?" A teacher who knows can pair you with a regular for the first rotation, which is the moment most solo beginners decide whether to come back.
  • Ask how you catch up. Some schools share a short video of the week's routine or run an open practice hour. For someone whose evenings sometimes run late, a video of the missed class is what makes "flexible schedules" true.
  • Ask about an end-of-year show. Many dance schools put one on. Ask whether it's optional and whether costumes cost extra. For some people it's an unwanted expense; for others it's the reason to sign up.
  • Get on the waiting list for your role. If the class is short of your role, ask to be called when a place opens, and whether you'd get in straight away if you brought a friend who would dance the other role.
  • Hold the place. "If the trial goes well, can you keep my place in the Thursday class until the next day so I can enroll online?"
  • Mention any injury now. A bad knee or back changes which style is sensible: partner dances load the knees with constant pivots, and some solo styles go down to the floor. The teacher can tell you which class suits your joints before you've paid for a term of the wrong one.
  • Ask what comes after beginner. The ad's "improve your skills" means there are higher levels. Ask whether your beginner group moves up together, which matters if you end up with people you like dancing with.

They see the food before they commit. "Discover our varied menus" means no blind bet. Your friend reads the menus tonight, holds them against the guest list (the cousin who doesn't eat meat, the colleague who hates fish) and picks the one that works for the whole table. If a menu can be adjusted, good; if not, the variety is what lets one menu fit everyone. The risk of the evening shrinks to something they can read on a screen.

This is the cheapest time to try it. The 10% is for new clients, so it's a one-time saving, and this party is the occasion that matters, which makes it the right booking to spend it on. Make it concrete only with the real price in front of you: "10% on dinner for ten is about what the wine costs" can be said once you've seen the number, never before.

Their hesitations

  1. "That must cost a fortune." The ad gives no price, so don't pretend to know one. "No idea. Let's look, it's on the site." Then compare with what the evening costs them anyway: groceries for ten, a full day in the kitchen, the cleanup the next morning. Fixes: fewer guests, a shorter menu if one exists, the 10%, and you paying half if you co-host. If the party is for someone's birthday, the guests chipping in for the chef can be the gift.
  2. "My kitchen is tiny" or "my oven is old." This company's whole idea is a chef in someone else's kitchen; kitchens they've never seen are their normal workplace. Look on the site for what the chef needs (burners, oven, fridge space) and describe the kitchen in the request. If it really won't work, you find out in five minutes, before anyone is invited.
  3. "I don't want a stranger running my house." The chef has the kitchen. The rest of the house and the evening stay your friend's: who sits where, the music, when dessert comes. Look for chef profiles or reviews on the site. Offer to arrive before the chef, so your friend isn't alone with someone new while getting ready.
  4. "What if it's bad, with everyone watching?" The ad says "professional cook", and the menus can be read before booking. Read the reviews together if the site has them. If this evening is high stakes (the in-laws, an anniversary), suggest a dinner for four first as a test, and say the trade out loud: the 10% goes on the test instead of the party.
  5. "Shouldn't the host do the cooking?" "Take care of everything" covers the kitchen. Hosting is everything else: the welcome, the drinks, the table, the toast. The guests come to see your friend, and for once your friend will be in the room with them.
  6. "I don't even have a date." That's what this conversation is for. "Book" means dates go. Pick two possible Saturdays now; the site will show which one a chef can do.

How you steer

Open with their wish, then the ad: "You keep saying you want to have everyone over. Look at this: 'A Chef in your kitchen... Our Chefs take care of everything!' You wouldn't cook at all."

Then listen to the first thing they say. "How much?" means price is the gate: stop arguing and open the site. "My kitchen's too small" means they're already picturing it; answer and keep going. A joke about it being fancy is interest; give them the scene on the sofa. A flat "maybe" means you've misread the evening: ask what kind of party they picture, because thirty people standing changes everything you check.

Stop adding reasons at their first practical question (how much, how far ahead, how many people). From there the site answers, and you read it with them in this order: whether they come to your friend's area (if not, it's over in thirty seconds), what "everything" includes, the price for ten, the menus, how far ahead to book, then the request form.

The close: "Grab your laptop. Let's open the site, look at the menus and the price for ten, and if it works, pick a Saturday and send the request tonight with the 10%." If they want the guests' answers first, settle that instead: they text everyone two dates tonight, and you fill in the request together tomorrow at lunch with the headcount.

How far it can go

  • Put the menu on the invitation. Once a menu is picked, the invitation writes itself: "Saturday at 8, a chef in my kitchen, here's the menu." People reply faster to an invitation with a menu on it, and anyone who can't eat something says so before the booking is final, which is exactly what the chef needs to know.
  • Co-host. Offer to handle the guest list and the dietary questions, bring the drinks (the ad says nothing about drinks), and split the bill. That cuts your friend's two biggest fears, work and money, in half before they've said yes.
  • Stretch "everything". Setting the table, serving, a second person for a bigger group: the ad lists none of these, so ask in the request what "everything" can include.
  • Use "Our Chefs" both ways. If the first chef is taken on their date, ask for another; if the evening works, ask for the same chef next time.
  • Read the 10% closely. Does it cover the whole bill, groceries included? Does it expire? Is it per household? If it's per person, you're a new client too, and the same discount works for your own dinner later.
